# L3 analysis: the case with many transparent points

Status: working memo, 2026-10-31. Owner of the investigation: Peyton Wang. Claude drafts; Codex reviews.

## Summary

The bounded theorem caps the number of transparent points `k`, and `k` measures the room's size, not its complexity. Integer scaling leaves a room dark and keeps `W` fixed, while it multiplies `k`. So an unbounded proof has to work with a quantity that scaling leaves unchanged.

This memo proposes that quantity: the unfolded translation surface, with its corners marked. For a room with `W` walls, the surface's genus and its number of marked points are bounded by functions of `W`. That makes the problem finite-dimensional for each `W`.

Lemmas 1 to 3 are proved here, with corrections from Codex's review (`illumination-k4-codex/proofs/l3-foundations-review.md`). Theorem 6, local rigidity of your room, is proved and independently checked. The global reduction is not proved, and the sections that follow state what it still needs.

## Lemma 1: the unfolded surface of an orthogonal room

**Statement.** Let `P` be a strictly simple orthogonal room with `W` walls, `c` convex corners, and `r` reflex corners. Glue 4 reflected copies of `P` along matching walls, using the group generated by the reflections in `x` and in `y`. The result is a closed translation surface `X` with these properties:

1. `X` has area `4 * area(P)`. If the corners of `P` lie on the integer grid, `X` is tiled by unit squares, so it is a square-tiled surface. For arbitrary real edge lengths it need not be.
2. Each corner of `P` lifts to exactly one point of `X`. A convex corner lifts to a regular point. A reflex corner lifts to a cone point of angle `6π`.
3. `X` has genus `g = r + 1 = (W - 2) / 2`.
4. An interior point of `P` has 4 lifts, and a point inside a wall has 2.
5. Interior points `s` and `t` form a dark pair exactly when the following holds. For each lift `t'` of `t`, every straight segment in `X` from `s` to `t'` meets a lift of a corner before it reaches `t'`.

**Proof.**
- Around a corner of angle `π/2`, the 4 copies supply a total angle of `2π`, so they close up to one regular point.
- Around a corner of angle `3π/2`, the copies supply `6π`, which forms one cone point.
- Gauss–Bonnet gives `2g - 2 = Σ(cone angle / 2π - 1) = 2r`, so `g = r + 1`.
- An orthogonal polygon has `c - r = 4`, so `W = 2r + 4`.
- Item 5 is the standard unfolding. A billiard path becomes a straight segment in `X`, and the stopping rule at corners becomes the condition on corner lifts.

**Check on your room.** It has 13 convex and 9 reflex corners, area 15, and genus 10. Its surface `X` has area 60 and 22 marked points.

## Lemma 2: the 45° case

For a room with diagonal walls, the reflections generate the symmetry group of the square, which has 8 elements, so `X` has 8 copies. A corner of angle `aπ/4` lifts as follows:

| `a` | Corner angle | Points over the corner | Cone angle of each |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 45° | 1 | `2π` |
| 2 | 90° | 2 | `2π` |
| 3 | 135° | 1 | `6π` |
| 5 | 225° | 1 | `10π` |
| 6 | 270° | 2 | `6π` |
| 7 | 315° | 1 | `14π` |

**Proof.** The 8 copies supply a total angle of `8 · aπ/4 = 2aπ`. A corner angle of `π · m/n` in lowest terms has cone angle `2πm`. Dividing the total by the cone angle gives `gcd(a, 4)` points.

So `X` has at most `2W` marked points, and `g = 1 + Σ(a - gcd(a, 4))/2` over the corners. An earlier draft used 16 copies and doubled every count; Codex caught the error. Darkness has the same form as Lemma 1, item 5.

## Lemma 3: scaling doesn't change the surface

**Statement.** Scale `P` by a positive integer `M`. The new surface is the image of `X` under the homothety by `M`. After you divide its charts by `M`, it is `X` itself, tiled by squares of side `1/M`. The marked points, `s`, and the lifts of `t` all keep their places.

**Proof.** Scaling commutes with reflection in lines through the origin. It maps the gluing of `P` onto the gluing of `MP`.

**Consequence.** Darkness is a property of the marked surface `(X, corners, s, lifts of t)` up to scaling. The tiling only says which surfaces arise. That's why bounds on `k` can't settle the general case, and why bounds on genus and marked points can.

Lemma 3 also explains Codex's finding about orthogonal rooms. The map `(x, y) -> (Mx, y)` commutes with both axis reflections, so it maps `X` to an affinely equivalent surface. It preserves straight lines and billiard connections, and therefore darkness. It isn't an isometry, and it doesn't map the integer lattice onto itself.

## A known constraint: marks are isolated

Three results apply. The citations were checked on 2026-10-31 against the arXiv listings:

- Hubert, Schmoll, and Troubetzkoy, "Modular fibers and illumination problems," *IMRN* 2008, arXiv:math/0602394. On a Veech surface, the points not illuminated from a given point form a finite set. The surfaces in Lemma 1 are square-tiled, so they are Veech surfaces.
- Lelièvre, Monteil, and Weiss, "Everything is illuminated," *Geometry & Topology* 20(3) (2016) 1737–1762, arXiv:1407.2975. They remove the Veech hypothesis. In a rational polygon, every point illuminates all but finitely many points.
- Wolecki, "Illumination in rational billiards," arXiv:1905.09358. In a rational polygon, only finitely many pairs of points fail to illuminate each other.

So, for a fixed room, you can't slide `t` and keep it dark. Darkness is a rigid, lattice-type coincidence. Theorem 6 is a parameter-space version of this fact, because it lets the walls move as well. None of the three papers gives the uniform bound on reflection words that the next-steps list asks for. Before citing them in a write-up, check their conventions for rays that reach a corner.

## Parameter space for a fixed shape

Fix a **shape** `τ`: the cyclic sequence of turn angles at the corners. With `W <= 21`, there are finitely many shapes.

- Rooms of shape `τ` are given by their edge lengths `ℓ in R^W`. These satisfy two linear closing equations, plus open inequalities that keep the room simple. Call this region `K_τ`. It is a polyhedral cone.
- Add the 4 coordinates of `s` and `t`. The full parameter space is a cone of dimension `W + 2`.
- Fix a reflection word `w`, the sequence of walls a ray hits. The copy of `t` that `w` gives depends linearly on the parameters. The conditions that the segment meets each wall in its interior are determinants of such vectors, so they are quadratic. The set lit through `w` is therefore an open semialgebraic set, not a polyhedral cone. Codex's example: in an L-shaped room, the marks `(-1, a)` and `(b, -1)` see each other directly exactly when `ab < 1`.
- The dark set `D_τ` is what remains after removing the countably many lit sets. It is closed and invariant under scaling. By the result of Lelièvre, Monteil, and Weiss, it has empty interior.

## Conjecture 4: rigidity

**Conjecture.** For each shape `τ` with `W <= 21`, `D_τ` is contained in a finite union of rational linear subspaces of positive codimension. On each one, darkness is a property of the subspace, not of the particular point.

**Why it alone wouldn't close L3.** A rational subspace contains infinitely many primitive integral points that aren't multiples of each other, such as `(1, n, 0)` in the plane `z = 0`. So containment in finitely many subspaces doesn't bound the size of a dark room. The constancy clause would have to be made precise and proved. Then you'd still need an explicit list of the subspaces, one representative of each, and a proof that each representative has `k <= 4` or a separate exclusion.

**Evidence.**
- Every known dark room comes from an exact lattice coincidence. Tokarsky's rooms, Wolecki's rooms, and your room all fit this pattern.
- Theorem 6 proves that small deformations of your room that aren't axis-affine maps light it. Codex's earlier strip test showed only that one way of carrying paths over fails. It didn't show the modified room is lit.

**Main risk.** The lit set is a union of infinitely many semialgebraic pieces. Their boundaries could pile up and leave a dark set that isn't a finite union of subspaces. To rule that out, you'd need a uniform bound on the reflection words that matter for each `τ`.


## Lemma 5: blocking at a corner breaks one-sidedly

**Setting.** Fix a shape `τ`. Deform a dark configuration along a line `θ0 + λθ` in parameter space, where `θ` keeps the polygon closed. Take a billiard path from `s` to `t`, at `λ = 0`, that touches exactly one corner `c` and otherwise reflects at wall interiors. Call it an **event**. Unfold the path. On the side `σ` of `c` that the path passes, `s`, the image `c'`, and the image `t'_σ` are collinear. All three move linearly with the parameters.

**Statement.** Let `f = ∇ det(t'_σ - s, c' - s)`, signed so that a valid path on side `σ` needs `det < 0` for left and `det > 0` for right. Write the result as a condition `f·θ >= 0` for darkness to survive. If `f·θ < 0`, the room is lit for all small `λ > 0`.

**Proof.** Every other contact of the path is a transversal hit inside a wall, or the arrival at `t`. Both are open conditions. Near an orthogonal corner, the local reflection pattern of a ray passing at distance `η` doesn't depend on `η`, so the side-`σ` continuation stays valid. `det(λ) = λ(f·θ) + O(λ²)`, so the segment passes `c` on side `σ` for small `λ > 0`.

At a convex 45° or 90° corner, the continuation through the regular point retraces the incoming path. So it can't join two distinct points (Wolecki, Remark 2.2). No events of this kind exist, and none occur in the certificates.

## Theorem 6: the 22-wall room is locally rigid

**Statement.** There is a neighborhood of your room, with its marks, in the parameter space of its shape. Every configuration in that neighborhood is lit, except the images of your room under maps `(x, y) -> (ax + b, cy + d)` with `a, c > 0`.

**Proof.**
1. `rigidity/rigidity_check.py` traces rays from `s` in every primitive direction `(p, q)` with `|p|, |q| <= 12`. The tracing uses exact rationals and branches on both sides of each reflex corner. It finds 147 events and no lit path. All 147 events are at reflex corners.
2. On the 26-dimensional closure subspace, the 147 functionals have rank 22. That equals 26 minus the 4 symmetry directions, and every functional vanishes on those directions.
3. `rigidity/posspan.py` finds a strictly positive rational combination of the functionals that equals zero, and checks it exactly. File: `rigidity/posspan-R12.json`. A set of vectors with this property positively spans its linear span. So for every `θ` outside the symmetry directions, some event has `f·θ < 0`, and Lemma 5 lights the room.
4. The set of unit directions modulo symmetries is compact, so `max_e(-f_e·θ)` has a positive lower bound on it. The second-order terms are uniformly bounded over finitely many events. Together these give one neighborhood for all directions.

**Check.** `rigidity/validate.py` perturbs the room along 6 random closure-preserving directions with `λ = 10^-5`. It traces each event's predicted path in the perturbed room with exact rationals. All 881 predictions agree, both lit and blocked.

With directions up to 6 only, the rank is 22, but no positive dependency exists. Small directions alone don't certify rigidity.

## What Theorem 6 means for L3

- Your room is an isolated point of its shape's dark set, up to axis-affine maps. Codex accepted this from an independent implementation (`illumination-k4-codex/rigidity-review/findings.md`). It matched all 147 events and their signs, and it gave a careful proof of the uniform neighborhood on a normalized slice. In particular, small strip insertions light the room.
- The same certificate works for any dark room. It is a fast, exact, local test: about 10 seconds per room.
- The global version is still open. It would need every dark point of `D_τ` to be rigid, plus control of `D_τ` near degenerate shapes. Near a degenerate shape, some edge becomes short compared to the others, which is where rooms with many transparent points live.
- Paper route for the degenerate end: when an edge shrinks, the room converges to a room of a simpler shape. A robust lit path of the limit room lights every nearby room. So dark rooms can pile up only at limits that are dark in a weak sense, with fewer walls or with contacts. That suggests an induction on `W`.

## Degenerate limits

This section handles rooms near the boundary of a shape's parameter cone. After scaling to unit diameter, such a room has an edge, a gap, or a mark distance that is small compared to the room. Rooms with many transparent points live here, because a primitive lattice room needs a large scale to fit a short feature.

### Lemma 7: robust lit paths persist

**Statement**, in the form Codex accepted. A finite reflection word joins distinct marks in a limit configuration `P0`. Its first and last directions lie strictly inside the marks' admissible sectors. Every reflection is transverse and strictly inside a wall. The path avoids all corners, contacts, and collapsed features. Along the path, nearby rooms have the same interior side and corresponding walls, with converging endpoints and supporting lines. Then every close enough configuration has a connection with the same word.

**Proof.** Unfold the word. Aim the new path at the moved copy of `t`, not along the old direction, because arrival at a fixed target isn't an open condition for a fixed ray. The conditions that remain are finitely many strict inequalities, so they persist.

### Proposition 8: limits of dark rooms are weakly dark

**Statement.** Let `P_n` be dark rooms of a fixed shape that converge, after normalization, to `P0`. Then `P0` has no robust liftable connection: no path that satisfies the hypotheses of Lemma 7. Call that **weakly dark**.

**Proof.** A robust connection would light `P_n` for large `n`, by Lemma 7.

Weak darkness isn't ordinary darkness. You must treat these cases separately: marks staying distinct and interior, a mark reaching the boundary, marks merging at the boundary, the interior disconnecting, and a corridor collapsing to a segment. Codex lists them in `illumination-k4-codex/degeneration-review/findings.md`. A precise version needs a decorated limit that keeps the wall faces, not only a list of blocker points.

**The marks can't merge in the interior.** Suppose `|s_n - t_n|` is small compared to the distance from the marks to the boundary. Then the straight segment between them lies inside the room and lights it. So the marks can only merge at a point of the boundary, which joins `Σ`.

### What the limits look like

| Collapse | Limit `P0` | Blocker |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A short edge, or a small notch on a wall | Fewer walls | A point on a wall |
| Two walls close to touching | A pinch contact | The contact point |
| A thin corridor | A slit, or a hole joined by a slit | Slit tips |
| A mark near the boundary | A mark on a wall | The mark |

Define the **weight** of a limit as its wall count, plus the fewest walls that any simple room near it must add. A small notch adds at least 2 walls. A thickened slit adds at least 2. A corridor to a hole adds at least 4. These are counts for single constructions. They don't yet prove additive costs for arbitrary degenerations, and they don't give a quantity that strictly decreases for an induction.

### Conjecture 9: no light dark limits

**Counterexample to the first version.** The first draft asked that no blocked limit of weight 21 or less is dark. Codex refuted it with an 8-wall family: `[-1,e]² ∪ [0,1]²` with marks `(-1/2,-1/2)` and `(1/2,1/2)`. Each room is simple and lit. As `e -> 0`, the limit is two squares that touch at one point. With that point as a blocker, the limit is dark, because its interior is disconnected.

**Conjecture, narrowed.** No sequence of dark simple rooms with at most 21 walls has a degenerate limit. A proof must exclude disconnected limits and marks merging at the boundary directly. Weight alone can't do that.

**Evidence.** The known dark rooms that aren't simple all have weight at least 22. Wolecki's pinch room has 22 walls. Tokarsky's post room has 18 walls and needs a corridor to the post, which adds at least 4. The 20-wall slit room needs at least 2 more walls to thicken its slit. Earlier bounded searches, recorded in `.context/illumination-analysis.md`, exclude slit rooms with fewer than 20 walls and contact rooms with fewer than 22, inside a 288-tile window.

### How L3 would follow

1. Fix a shape with at most 21 walls.
2. Normalize away every symmetry, including both axis scalings for orthogonal shapes. Unit diameter alone isn't enough.
3. By the narrowed Conjecture 9, dark rooms of that shape stay a fixed distance from the boundary of the normalized region.
4. On that compact region, if every real dark configuration is isolated, the dark set is compact and discrete, and so finite. Codex accepts this step once its hypotheses are proved.
5. Finitely many dark rooms have some maximum number of transparent points. That number isn't explicit, and it isn't automatically 4. A bounded theorem with an explicit larger cap would need its own reduction and its own checked exclusion.

Steps 3 and 4 each rest on an unproved claim. Step 4 needs every real dark configuration to be isolated, not only your room and the 24-wall room. Irrational accumulation points must be handled too. It may be possible to prove that from the parity structure of Lemma 1, because every blocking corner sits at an odd lattice step. Step 3 needs the narrowed Conjecture 9, which has the same form as the original question. The difference is that its rooms carry extra blockers and count walls by weight, so an induction on weight may work.

## Separating pinches

This section starts on the first exclusion that the narrowed Conjecture 9 needs. The setting: dark simple rooms `P_n` converge to a limit `P0`, in which two boundary arcs touch at a point `z`. Removing `z` separates `P0` into a part `A` that contains `s` and a part `B` that contains `t`.

**Neck hypothesis.** Near `z`, assume that `P_n` is two wedges joined by a **window**: a segment of length `ε_n -> 0` between two corners, which cuts `P_n` into an `s` side and a `t` side. The local model is the same for all `n`, scaled by `ε_n`. Necks with more structure at scale `ε_n`, such as a short corridor, aren't covered yet.

### Lemma 10: crossing fans light the room

**Setting.** Put coordinates on the window, `w in [0, ε]`. Develop both sides into one chart that contains the window. An **s-fan** is a reflection word with this property: for every `w` in the window, the path from `s` that follows the word to the window point `w` is unobstructed. Each such path reflects transversally inside walls and touches no corner. Its developed direction `δ` then ranges over an interval `I_s`, and `w` is a strictly monotone function of `δ`. A **t-fan** is defined the same way, from `t`, with interval `I_t`.

**Statement.** If `I_s` and `I_t` overlap in an interval of positive length, then `s` and `t` illuminate each other.

**Proof.**
- In the developed chart, `s'` and `t'` lie on opposite sides of the window line.
- As `δ` increases, the crossing point of the line from `s'` moves one way along the window, and the crossing point of the line to `t'` moves the other way. So `f(δ) = w_s(δ) - w_t(δ)` is strictly increasing on the overlap.
- At the left end of the overlap, either `w_s = 0` or `w_t = ε`, so `f <= 0`. At the right end, either `w_s = ε` or `w_t = 0`, so `f >= 0`.
- So `f` has exactly one zero. It's strictly inside the overlap, because a zero at an end would force the overlap to be a single point.
- At that zero, the two lines meet at one interior point of the window with one direction. Their union is a billiard path from `s` to `t` that crosses the window without reflecting. No corner lies on it.

### Corollary 11: the first-order test at a pinch

**Setting.** Suppose `P0` has a straight path through `z`. That is an `s`-word that reaches `z` in developed direction `δ0`, and a `t`-word that leaves `z` in the same direction. Each is robust except at `z`. Let `L_s` and `L_t` be the developed lengths of the two halves. In the developed chart of `P_n`, put the window on the line `x = 0`, with `s'_n = (-a_n, y_s)` and `t'_n = (b_n, y_t)`, where `a_n, b_n > 0`. Let `m0` be the slope of `δ0`. Then `c_s = y_s + a_n·m0` and `c_t = y_t - b_n·m0` are where the direction-`δ0` lines from `s'_n` and to `t'_n` cross the window line.

**Statement.** For large `n`, `P_n` is lit whenever the exact crossing point lies in the open window:

    0 < w_n = (b_n·c_s + a_n·c_t) / (a_n + b_n) < ε_n.

If `c_s/ε_n` and `c_t/ε_n` stay bounded, the limit weights can replace the exact ones. Then `P_n` is lit for large `n` when `lim (L_t·c_s + L_s·c_t)/((L_s + L_t)·ε_n)` lies in `(0, 1)`.

**Proof.** `w_n` is where the segment from `s'_n` to `t'_n` crosses `x = 0`, by linear interpolation. For large `n`, the two words develop across the whole window, and each path stays close to its limit. That limit is a positive distance from every other corner. The neck hypothesis keeps the paths inside the two wedges near `z`. So the segment is a billiard path when `0 < w_n < ε_n`. For the second form, `a_n/(a_n + b_n)` converges to `L_s/(L_s + L_t)`. The error in `w_n/ε_n` is that difference times the bounded offsets `c/ε_n`, so it tends to zero.

**Codex's correction.** An earlier version used the limit weights with no bound on the offsets. Codex gave two exact counterexamples in `degeneration-review/pinch-check.py`. In the first, the weighted prediction is `ε³/2`, inside the window, but the exact crossing is negative. In the second, the offsets are of order `ε/h` with `ε = h²`. The prediction is `1/4` of the window, but the exact crossing is negative. Both counterexamples refute only the old proof, not the possibility that another word lights the room.

**Reading.** `c_s`, `c_t`, and `ε_n` are all first order in the deformation. So a dark sequence must keep a weighted average of two offsets outside the window, for every straight path through `z`. Different paths give different weights `L_t : L_s`. In Codex's 8-wall family, the diagonal through `z` has `c_s = c_t = ε/2`, so the test lights every room in the family.

### What is still open at a pinch

- **No straight path through `z`.** The limit `P0` might have no `s`-word and `t`-word with the same direction at `z`. Then every lit path of `P_n` uses words whose length grows as `ε_n -> 0`, and Corollary 11 doesn't apply.
- **A heuristic count.** Suppose that the paths from `s` to `z` of length at most `L` number about `L²`, and their directions spread out evenly, and the same holds for `t`. Then the number of pairs whose directions agree to within `ε/L` grows like `ε·L³`. That's unbounded for every fixed `ε`, which suggests that the room is lit.
- **The gap in the heuristic.** Lemma 10 needs fans that cross the whole window. A long path passes within about `1/L` of other corners, and those corners cut a fan of width `ε` once `L` is larger than about `1/ε`. A proof needs a count of "fat" paths: paths with a corner-free margin of width `ε` around them. I don't know a reference for such a count. It is the next thing to look for.
- **Other neck types.** Short corridors, and contacts between a corner and the inside of a wall, need their own local models.

### Lemma 12: points in one cylinder see each other

**Setting.** Let `P` be a room with corners on the integer grid, and marks `s` and `t` at lattice points. Let `X` be its unfolded surface. Treat every lift of a corner as a marked point. Fix a rational direction `(p, q)`, with `p` and `q` coprime integers. Then `X` splits into open cylinders, which are flat annuli foliated by closed leaves in that direction. The boundaries of the cylinders are unions of segments between marked points.

**Statement.**
1. Suppose that `s` and a lift `t'` of `t` lie in the same open cylinder. Then `s` and `t` illuminate each other.
2. `P` is lit exactly when, for some rational direction, one of two things holds. Either `s` and a lift of `t` lie in a common open cylinder. Or they lie on a common boundary leaf with no marked point between them.
3. Every cylinder in direction `(p, q)` has height at least `1/√(p² + q²)`. A lattice point inside a cylinder lies at least that far from its boundary.

**Proof.**
1. The open cylinder is isometric to an open strip `R × (0, h)` modulo a translation. Lift `s` and `t'` into the strip. The straight segment between the lifts stays in the open strip, because the strip is convex. So it avoids the boundary, and with it every marked point.
2. The "if" direction is item 1, or the boundary segment itself. For "only if", take a lighting segment from `s` to `t'`. Its direction `t' - s` is an integer vector, because `s` and `t` are lattice points and reflections in tiling lines map lattice points to lattice points. Without lattice marks, this step fails. In that direction every leaf is either closed or a boundary leaf, and the segment lies in one leaf.
3. Marked points are lattice points, so boundary leaves lie on lattice lines in direction `(p, q)`. Those lines are spaced `1/√(p² + q²)` apart.

Lemma 12 is a standard fact about translation surfaces. I haven't found it stated for this use.

**What it changes, as Codex corrected it.** Item 3 gives an exact clearance for each fixed cylinder on a fixed grid. It does not remove the fan-cutting concern. After you scale `P_n` to unit size, the grid spacing is `h_n`, and the bound becomes `h_n/√(p² + q²)`. That tends to zero as the grid refines or the direction grows. So item 3 gives no uniform clearance at the scale of the neck, and no count of cylinders that contain both marks.

**The estimate a proof needs, in grid units.** The window is a lattice segment, so `ε_n ≥ h_n`. A lighting segment between lifts of A marks has displacement `2k(p, q)·h_n`, so its length is `L = 2k·|(p, q)|·h_n`. A cylinder in that direction is at least `h_n/|(p, q)| = 2k·h_n²/L` high. For `L` of order 1, that is of order `h_n²`, which is much smaller than the neck. The needed statement is a count of open cylinders, or boundary leaves, that cross the neck and hold both marks. It must hold in units of `h_n`, uniformly in `n`.

### Conjecture 13: a cylinder crosses the neck

**Conjecture.** Let `P_n` be grid rooms that converge, after scaling, to a limit with a separating pinch. Then for large `n`, some rational direction has a cylinder that crosses the neck and contains both `s` and a lift of `t`. By Lemma 12, `P_n` is then lit.

Codex notes that this is stronger than illumination. Lemma 12 also allows a lighting segment along a boundary leaf. So a weaker conjecture, "an open cylinder or a boundary leaf", would also suffice.

**Why it's plausible.**
- A cylinder that crosses the neck has all its leaves crossing it. A leaf can't pass through the neck's corners, so the cylinder can't widen past them.
- Cylinders of circumference at most `L` number about `L²` (Eskin–Masur; Veech for Veech surfaces), and square-tiled surfaces are Veech surfaces. Those that cross the neck and contain `s` are a fraction of them.
- Each such cylinder has area at least its height times its circumference. That gives a positive chance of containing one of the lifts of `t`.

**Main risk.** The counting constants depend on the surface. As `n` grows, the grid gets finer compared with the room, and the surfaces change. A proof needs counts that hold uniformly along the sequence. That's where a theorem, rather than a heuristic, is still missing.

**Related literature.** arXiv:1905.09312, on Teichmüller curves in genus two, states an illumination conjecture for square-tiled surfaces, with light sources at cusps. It shows that a vertex leaves only rational points of the squares unlit. It's worth reading for methods.

## Necks of other shapes

Corollary 11 assumed the simplest neck: two wedges joined by a window. The same first-order test works for any neck, as follows.

**Local model.** Scale `P_n` near `z` by `1/ε_n`. Assume the scaled neck converges to a fixed local model `M`. Examples:
- a window between two corners;
- a short corridor;
- a corner tip near the inside of a wall.

Fix a local word: the sequence of walls of `M` that a path crosses the neck by, possibly with no reflection. For a direction `δ0`, the **passage interval** `J(δ0)` is the set of transverse offsets of lines in direction `δ0` that cross `M` from the `s` side to the `t` side by that word. They must touch no corner and reflect transversally. `J(δ0)` is open.

### Corollary 14: the first-order test for any neck

**Statement.** Suppose `P0` has a path that reaches `z` from `s` in developed direction `δ0`, robust except at `z`. Suppose also a path from `z` to `t` leaves in direction `δ0` after developing through the local word, and is robust except at `z`. Let `c_s` and `c_t` be the transverse offsets of the direction-`δ0` lines from `s'_n` and to `t'_n`, measured at the neck in units of `ε_n`. If

    ρ = lim (L_t·c_s + L_s·c_t) / (L_s + L_t)

lies in `J(δ0)`, then `P_n` is lit for large `n`. This needs two more hypotheses, which Codex added:
- The offsets `c_s` and `c_t`, in units of `ε_n`, stay bounded.
- The labeled walls of the local word, and their interior sides, converge to those of `M`, as in the corrected Lemma 7. Hausdorff convergence alone doesn't give this.

**Proof.** Develop the `s` path, the local word, and the `t` path into one chart. The segment from `s'_n` to `t'_n` crosses the neck at the exact weighted offset, with weights from the current distances `a_n` and `b_n`. Because the offsets are bounded, that converges to `ρ`, as in Corollary 11. Its direction differs from `δ0` by `O(ε_n)`. `J(δ0)` is open, so for large `n` the segment crosses `M` by the local word. Away from the neck, the segment stays close to the robust limit paths. So it is a billiard path from `s` to `t`.

This direct argument also reproves Corollary 11 without Lemma 10. Lemma 10 is still needed when the words grow with `n`.

**The neck shapes.**
- **Window.** `J(δ0)` is the window seen along `δ0`. That gives Corollary 11.
- **Short corridor.** `J(δ0)` is nonempty for the no-reflection word exactly when `δ0` points through the corridor without hitting its sides. For other directions, words that reflect off the corridor walls take over. Each word is a local model of its own.
- **Corner near a wall.** Lines pass through the gap between the tip and the wall, directly or after one reflection off the wall near the gap. Each gives an open passage interval.

So the first-order test at a pinch doesn't depend on the neck's shape. What remains open, for every neck shape, is the case with no straight path through `z`, which is Conjecture 13.

## Other degenerations

This section covers the remaining cases in Codex's list: a mark reaching the boundary, marks merging at the boundary, a pinch that doesn't separate, and a short wall collapsing. Throughout, `P_n` are dark simple rooms of one shape that converge to `P0` after full normalization. The marks converge, `s_n -> s0` and `t_n -> t0`. Write `α` for the interior angle at a corner. A point inside a wall counts as a corner with `α = π`.

### Lemma 15: a mark near a corner

**Setting.** Suppose `s_n -> c`, where `c` is a corner of `P0` with angle `α`. Let `c_n` be the corresponding corner of `P_n`, so `c_n -> c`. Measure angles at `c_n` from one of its walls, so the room near `c_n` is the sector `(0, α)`. Let `r_n = |s_n - c_n|`, and let `φ_s` be the limit of the angle of `s_n - c_n`, after passing to a subsequence. It lies in `[0, α]`. Assume the room is exactly that sector within distance `K·r_n` of `c_n`, for every fixed `K` and large `n`. That means no other feature of `P_n` degenerates at the scale `r_n`.

**Codex's correction.** An earlier version measured angles from the limit point `c`, not from the moving corner `c_n`. Codex gave an exact counterexample in `degeneration-review/other-degenerations-check.py`. With `c_n = (-h, 0)` and `s_n = c_n + h²(-1, -2)`, the angle seen from `c` passes the test. But the prescribed segment leaves the sector at `c_n`. That refutes the old proof, not illumination by another word.

**Statement.** Suppose `P0` has a path from `c` to `t0` that leaves `c` in direction `ψ0`, strictly inside `(0, α)`. Suppose it's robust away from `c`, as in Lemma 7. If `|φ_s - ψ0| < π` and `ψ0 ≠ φ_s ± π`, then `P_n` is lit for large `n`. The same holds when `t0` is also a corner and the path is a loop from `c` back to `c`. The loop needs a developed limit segment of positive length, and the same moving-corner test at each end.

**Proof.**
1. Develop the path's word. Join `s_n` to the developed image of `t_n` by a segment. Its direction `δ_n` tends to `ψ0`.
2. Zoom by `1/r_n` around `c_n`. The room becomes the fixed sector, and `s_n` becomes a point `ŝ_n` with `|ŝ_n| = 1` and `ŝ_n -> ŝ`. By the setting, the zoomed room converges to the sector on every bounded region. The segment becomes a ray from `ŝ_n` with direction `δ_n`.
3. Seen from `c`, the angle of a point moving along a ray changes monotonically along the shorter arc, from its start angle to the ray's direction. Both `φ_s` and `ψ0` lie in `[0, α]`, and `|φ_s - ψ0| < π`. So the shorter arc stays inside `[0, α]`, and the limit ray from `ŝ` leaves the sector only at infinity.
4. The distance from that ray to the sector's walls grows linearly with the distance from `c`. If `ŝ` is on a wall, the ray leaves it transversally, because `ψ0` points strictly into the sector. Both properties survive the small changes `ŝ_n -> ŝ` and `δ_n -> ψ0`. So, for large `n`, the segment clears `c` and its walls at every scale.
5. Away from `c`, the segment stays close to the robust limit path, as in Lemma 7. So it's a billiard path from `s_n` to `t_n`.

Unlike Corollary 11, this test has no interpolation constraint. At the zoom scale, the direction error `δ_n - ψ0` only perturbs the ray slightly. Convergence of the aimed direction is enough. The strict contacts control the rest of the word.

### Corollary 16: marks at the boundary

Items 1 to 3 assume that both marks of `P_n` stay interior and that no other feature degenerates near the boundary point. Use the moving corner, as in Lemma 15.

1. **A mark tending to a wall interior or a convex corner.** Then `α <= π`, so `|φ_s - ψ0| < π` for every `ψ0` in `(0, α)`. Darkness of `P_n` forces `P0` to have no robust connection from `s0` at all. Wall points and convex 45° and 90° corners are regular points of `X(P0)`, by Lemma 2. A convex 135° corner isn't: its single lift has cone angle `6π`. So for 135° corners, the limit is a dark pair with an endpoint sector at a cone point, not at a regular point.
2. **Marks can't merge at a wall interior or a convex corner.** Near such a point, the room is a convex sector. If `s_n` and `t_n` both tend to it, the segment between them stays in that sector, so it lights `P_n`.
3. **Two marks on one wall.** If `s0` and `t0` lie inside the same wall, with no corner between them, the room contains a thin strip along that piece of the wall. The segment `s_n t_n` stays in that strip, so `P_n` is lit.
4. **Marks at a reflex corner can be locally dark.** At a 270° corner, put `t_n` at angle `0.1` and `s_n` at angle `1.4π`, both at distance `r_n -> 0`. On the unwrapped sheet at `c`, the images of `t_n` sit at angles `±0.1 + 2m·α`. Each is at angular distance more than `π` from `s_n`. So no path of the local model joins them. Lemma 15 then gives a necessary condition, not a classification: `P0` must have no robust loop from `c` to `c` whose departure angle is within `π` of `φ_s`, and whose arrival angle is within `π` of `φ_t`, in either order. Each mark's admissible arc has length at least `π`, out of `α <= 7π/4`.

So a mark that reaches a wall or convex corner gives a dark configuration of the same shape, in the boundary stratum. Each such configuration can be tested exactly with the certificate of Theorem 6, plus active boundary constraints, as Codex corrected it:
- At a wall, add `h`, the first-order distance from the mark to the moving wall. A normal applied only to the mark's coordinates isn't enough.
- At a convex corner, add one such constraint for each of its two walls.

If the enlarged list positively spans, no nearby normalized configuration with the marks inside is dark, except symmetric images. The proof is step 4 of Theorem 6, run on the closed cone `h >= 0`. It uses the same slice, quotient, and quadratic remainder, and it includes tangent directions. Positive spanning is sufficient, not necessary.

What's still open for marks at the boundary is the reflex-corner case. It needs robust loops at `c` in prescribed arcs, in a prescribed holonomy class. Saddle connections from a cone point have dense directions, but I don't know a result for loops from `c` back to `c`.

**A cylinder version of the reflex-corner case.** Lemma 12, item 1, holds on any translation surface, not only on the grid. That gives a second route that needs no exact loop. Suppose some direction `θ` of `X(P0)` splits into cylinders, the sector `(θ, θ + π)` at `c` contains the angle `φ_s`, and the cylinder adjacent to that sector contains `t0` in its interior. Then for large `n`, `s_n` and `t_n` lie in one open cylinder of `X(P_n)`, so `P_n` is lit. Cylinders persist under small deformations, with slightly changed direction and slightly smaller height. But the boundary can move by an amount of order `|P_n - P0|`. So the argument needs `s_n` to stay inside, which holds when the deformation is small compared with `r_n = |s_n - c_n|`. When it isn't, the argument fails. So the open question becomes: as `θ` runs over periodic directions, does the cylinder that contains `t0` ever touch `c` in the right sector? Equidistribution of long cylinders (Veech; Eskin–Masur) suggests a positive proportion do, but I haven't found a theorem. A search on saddle connections from a singularity back to itself found only general density results (Masur; Vorobets), not loops at a prescribed cone point.

### Proposition 17: a pinch that doesn't separate

**Statement.** Suppose the only degeneration is a contact at `z`, and `P0 \ {z}` stays connected. Assume the marks stay distinct and interior, and the labeled walls away from `z` converge face by face. Then `(s0, t0)` is dark in `P0`, with `z` as a blocker, and `P0` has at most `W` walls.

**Proof.** Away from `z`, the walls of `P_n` converge face by face. So every connection of `P0` that avoids corners and `z` satisfies Lemma 7, and it lights `P_n`. A billiard path inside `P0` can't graze a wall without running along it into a corner. So "no robust connection" is the same as ordinary darkness with `z` blocking. In the window model, the two corners of the window become the contact, so no wall is lost. A contact between a corner and the inside of a wall needs its own count of how the contact splits walls.

**What it leaves.** This case reduces to a sibling question: is any room with one point contact and at most 21 walls dark? Wolecki's pinch room has 22 walls and is the smallest known. The earlier bounded searches exclude contact rooms with fewer than 22 walls, inside a 288-tile window. The wall count doesn't drop, so this isn't an induction step. Corollary 14 adds first-order constraints at the window, but no contradiction yet.

### Proposition 18: a short wall collapses

**Statement.** Suppose the only degeneration is a wall `e_n` whose length tends to 0, with corners of angles `a1·π/4` and `a2·π/4` at its ends. Its two corners merge into one point `c` of `P0`, with angle `(a1 + a2 - 4)·π/4`.
Assume the marks stay distinct and interior, and nothing else degenerates.
1. If `0 < angle < 2π` and the angle isn't `π`, the two remaining neighbors aren't collinear. Then `P0` is a strictly simple room with `W - 1` walls, and its marks are dark in the ordinary sense.
2. If the angle is `π`, the wall was a small step. Its two neighbors merge into one wall, and `P0` is a strictly simple room with `W - 2` walls. Codex's example: the 6-wall room `(0,0), (2,0), (2,1), (2+h,1), (2+h,2), (0,2)` becomes a 4-wall rectangle as `h -> 0`. The point `z` where the step was is a collapsed feature, so robust paths of `P0` must avoid it. That makes `P0` dark only with `z` as an extra blocker. Lemma 19 below handles this case. The earlier claim that case 2 reduces to H(W - 2) alone was wrong.
3. If the angle is `0` or `2π`, the neighbors fold onto each other. The angle `0` comes from the end of a thin corridor, and `2π` from the tip of a thin slit. Two long walls approach each other there, so this is a cluster of collapsing edges, not one short wall. It is the corridor and slit case.

**Codex's correction.** An earlier version said that the angle `π` gives a notch. That's wrong: it's a smooth step, and the wall count drops by 2. A real shrinking notch collapses several edges at once, and its passage intervals must come from its own scaled local model.

**Proof of 1 and 2.** As in Proposition 17, robust connections of `P0` survive. The corner angles add as stated, because the turning angles add. In case 1 the neighbors stay distinct walls. In case 2 they are collinear and merge.

**What it gives.** Case 1 turns single-wall collapse into an induction on `W`, over real configurations. Case 2 leaves a blocker, so it needs the decorated version below.

**Codex's cost theorem.** `collapse-reduction.md`, Proposition 2, gives the right induction class: strictly simple rooms with `B` blockers on smooth boundary points, with integer cost `C = W + 2B`. Suppose a Jordan collapse removes `r` edges and creates `s` smooth points. Then `C' <= C - (r - s)`. Any collapsed chain of at least two edges lowers the cost, at any mix of rates. So does a one-edge collapse to a proper corner. The cost stays the same only when every collapsed chain is one edge that becomes a smooth step. Claude checked the bookkeeping: `s <= K <= r`, where `K` counts the chains, and `B' <= B + s`.

The induction claim becomes:

> **H(C).** No strictly simple 45° room with real parameters and smooth boundary blockers of total cost `C` has a dark pair of distinct interior points.

The cost-preserving smooth step is the one point collapse that this induction doesn't handle. Lemma 19's slab and Codex's step interval are the tools for it.

H(C - 1) excludes every Jordan point collapse except isolated smooth steps, when the marks stay distinct and interior. The proved bounded theorem covers only lattice rooms with at most 4 transparent points, so it doesn't supply H(C) for small `C`. Small cases still need a check over real configurations.

## Clusters of collapsing edges

A **cluster** is a set of edges of `P_n` that degenerate together. Two kinds occur:
- **Small clusters.** A chain of `m` consecutive edges, all of length `O(ε_n)`, lies in a ball of radius `K·ε_n` around a point `z`. Notches and steps are small clusters.
- **Long clusters.** Two long walls approach to distance `ε_n` along a segment of positive length. A thin corridor of the room, or a thin strip outside it, collapses to a segment. That gives a corridor or a slit in the limit.

Throughout, the marks stay distinct, and the walls away from the cluster converge face by face.

### Proposition 18′: a small cluster at a proper corner

**Statement.** Suppose a small cluster of `m` edges merges into a point `z` with angle `α`, where `0 < α < 2π` and `α ≠ π`. Suppose the marks stay distinct and interior. Suppose also, as Codex requires, that deleting the `m` collapsed edges leaves an injective Jordan cycle with no other contact or overlap. Codex's `degeneration-review/collapse-reduction.md`, Proposition 1, proves the general version. Then `P0` is a strictly simple room with `W - m` walls, and its marks are dark in the ordinary sense.

**Proof.** The turning angles add, so `z` is a corner of `P0` with angle `α`. The cluster's `m + 1` corners become 1 corner, so `m` walls are lost. Robust connections of `P0` avoid `z`, because `z` is a corner of `P0`. They light `P_n` by Lemma 7. So `P0` has no connection, which is ordinary darkness.

This gives the induction step H(C - m), for every `m >= 1`, with no blockers added.

### Lemma 19: a small cluster inside a wall

**Setting.** A small cluster merges into a point `z` inside a wall of `P0`, so `α = π`. Steps and notches are examples. Let `γ` be a connection of `P0` that reflects at `z`, not tangentially, and is robust everywhere else. Develop `γ` with a plain reflection in the wall line at `z`. Let `w_n` be the exact point where the segment from `s_n` to the developed image of `t_n` crosses that line, measured from the cluster's center.

**Statement.** If `P_n` is dark, then `|w_n| <= K_γ·ε_n` for large `n`. The constant `K_γ` depends only on the cluster radius and the angle between `γ` and the wall.

**Codex's correction to the proof.** The two outer faces of the cluster needn't lie on one line. A step has faces on `x = 0` and `x = ε`. So compare the reflected segment for each actual face with the one for a reference line. The faces are `O(ε_n)` apart, so transversality moves the contact by at most `C_γ·ε_n`. If the connection meets `z` more than once, track every visit in the same word. With those repairs, the argument below works.

**Proof.** Outside the ball of radius `K·ε_n` around `z`, each outer face of `P_n` is straight. A line that meets the wall line at distance more than `K·ε_n / sin(angle)` from the center misses the ball, both arriving and leaving. If `|w_n|` exceeds that, the segment reflects off the straight part of the wall. Away from `z` it stays close to `γ`, so it's a billiard path, as in Lemma 7.

This needs no local model. It holds for every cluster shape, because the passage set of the plain-reflection word contains everything outside a bounded interval.

**Dichotomy.** The limit `P0` has `W - m - 1` walls: the cluster's edges go, and its two neighbors merge. One of two things holds:
1. `P0` is dark in the ordinary sense, with `z` not needed as a blocker. That gives H(C - m - 1), with no blocker kept.
2. `P0` has connections, and every one passes through `z`. Then each gives a two-sided constraint `|g_γ(δ)| <= K_γ·ρ(δ) + O(|δ|²)`. Here `δ` is the deformation and `ρ` bounds the cluster size. Crossings are rational functions of the parameters, so this is a first-order bound with a remainder, not an exact linear test. A normalized cone certificate with a strict margin would still exclude the case, by a Taylor argument, as in Theorem 6.

**Codex's exact step interval.** For one smooth step, translate it to the origin, with faces `x = 0` below and `x = ε` above. Take developed source `(-a, u)` and target `(-b, v)`, with `u < 0 < v`, and put `K = b·u + a·v`. The lower-face word works exactly when `K < 0`. The upper-face word works exactly when `K > -2ε·u`. So both fail on `0 <= K <= -2ε·u`, an interval of width proportional to `ε`. Claude checked these formulas in 2,000 exact random cases. The slab is real and doesn't vanish after rescaling.

Case 2 asks for rooms where one wall point blocks every connection. Lelièvre–Monteil–Weiss allow regular marked points. So, for a fixed `P0`, `s0`, and `z`, only finitely many `t0` are dark with `z` blocking. That limits case 2 but doesn't exclude it, and it says nothing uniform as `z` moves along the wall.

### Lemma 20: the tip of a thin slit

**Setting.** A thin strip outside the room collapses to a slit of `P0`. The strip has one short tip edge with two 270° corners. Both faces of the slit reflect, and they converge face by face. Count the slit as two walls, one per face. Then `P0` has `W - 1` walls.

**Statement.**
1. Robust connections of `P0` reflect off either face and avoid the tip. They light `P_n`, by Lemma 7. So `P0` is dark as a slit room.
2. Let `γ` be a connection of `P0` that passes straight through the tip, not parallel to the slit, and is robust elsewhere. Let `w_n` be the exact crossing of the developed segment with the slit's line, measured past the tip. If `P_n` is dark, then `w_n <= K_γ·ε_n`.

**Proof of 2.** Past the tip by more than `K·ε_n`, the line misses the thickened tip. It's then a billiard path, as in Lemma 19.

Codex accepts this conditionally. Measure from a reference tip that moves with the strip. Bound the strip's thickness and the tip's motion uniformly. Fix the sign by which side of the tip is open. Retain any other collapsed smooth points as blockers.

So the tip acts as a 360° corner with a one-sided event, as in Lemma 5, but with slack of order `ε_n`. The slit case reduces to dark slit rooms with `W - 1` walls, counting faces. The earlier bounded searches found none with fewer than 20 walls inside a 288-tile window. That's evidence, not proof.

### Lemma 21: a mark in a thin dead-end corridor

**Setting.** A straight rectangular corridor of `P_n` has width `ε_n -> 0`, a fixed length `ℓ > 0`, and a dead end. Its mouth tends to a point `z` inside a wall of `P0`. The mark `s_n` is in the corridor, at depth `d` below the mouth, with `0 < d < ℓ`. Nothing else degenerates near `z`.

**Statement.** Let `γ` be a path of `P0` from `z` to `t0` that leaves `z` in direction `ψ0` in `(0, π)`, and is robust away from `z`. Develop `t_n` along `γ` to `t' = (X, Y)`, with the mouth on the line `y = 0`. If `Y < 2ℓ - d`, then `P_n` is lit for large `n`.

**Proof.**
1. Put the corridor in `0 < x < ε_n` and `y < 0`. A path from `s_n` leaves the corridor the first time it reaches `y = 0`. So only two depths of images are valid: `D = d`, for paths that go straight up, and `D = 2ℓ - d`, for paths that reflect once off the dead end first. Reflections in the sides allow any number of bounces, so at depth `D` the images sit at `x = ±x_s + 2k·ε_n`, for every integer `k`.
2. The segment from the image `(x*, -D)` to `t'` crosses `y = 0` at `x*·ρ + X·(1 - ρ)`, where `ρ = Y/(D + Y)`. As `k` runs over the integers, these crossings form two progressions with step `2ρ·ε_n`.
3. If `2ρ < 1`, which means `Y < D`, some crossing lies strictly inside the window `(0, ε_n)`. Take `D = 2ℓ - d`, the larger depth.
4. That image has `x* ≈ -X·D/Y`, so the segment's direction tends to the direction from `z` to `t'`, which is `ψ0`. Past the window, the segment follows `γ`, as in Lemma 15 with `α = π`.
5. **Dead-end corners, proved by Codex.** The end-reflected segment could hit a lift of a dead-end corner. Suppose every end-reflected candidate does. Then exactly two exist, of opposite signs, and their end crossings are consecutive integers. Those two incidences force the direct path from depth `d`, with no end reflection, to exit at the same mouth point as one of them. That path never reaches the dead end, so it touches no corner. See `degeneration-review/cluster-review.md`, "Repair of Opus's corrected strict-height criterion". Claude checked each identity by hand and reproduced Codex's exceptional example, where both end candidates hit corners and the direct path works. Claude also ran an exact brute force over 20,000 random rational cases, and every case had a valid path.

**Codex's counterexample to the earlier version.** Take a corridor of length 1 and the half-plane above it. Put `s_n = (ε_n/10, -1/2)` and the target at `(1, 10)`, with `ε_n = 1/(40n + 24)`. For every `n`, every integer `k`, both signs, and both valid depths, the segment misses the open mouth. So the plain exterior word never transfers. Here `Y = 10`, which is more than `2ℓ - d = 3/2`, so the corrected lemma doesn't claim this case. Claude checked the example by exact arithmetic for `n` from 0 to 5.

**Self-correction.** An earlier version used images at every depth `±d + 2jℓ`. That's wrong: a path that comes back up the corridor leaves through the mouth the first time it reaches it. With only two depths, the lemma needs `Y < 2ℓ - d`. When `Y >= 2ℓ - d`, the test becomes a condition modulo `2ρ·ε_n`, of the same kind as for a corridor that joins two parts.

**Consequence.** A mark deep in a thin corridor sees out of the mouth in every direction whose developed target is close enough to the mouth. Darkness of `P_n` forces `P0` to have no robust connection from `z` with `Y < 2ℓ - d`. Two simpler cases are settled. If both marks are in the corridor, the corridor is convex, so it's lit. If neither mark is in it, the mouth is a small feature inside a wall at scale `ε_n`, and Lemma 19 applies.

### What stays open for long clusters

- **A corridor that joins two parts.** Unfolded by its side reflections, the corridor becomes a full strip of height `ℓ`, and every line across it is a valid passage. Its only corner lifts sit on the two mouth lines, spaced `ε_n` apart. A path from `s0` to one mouth and a path from the other mouth to `t0` must match in direction. The test then becomes a condition on `ℓ·cot(δ0)` modulo `2ε_n`. For finitely many paths, a well-chosen sequence `ε_n` can fail all the conditions at once, by simultaneous Diophantine approximation. A proof needs infinitely many paths, as in Conjecture 13. This is the long version of the separating pinch.
- **Corridors that bend or end in diagonal walls.** Lemma 21 uses a straight rectangular dead end.

## A mark at a reflex corner

This section takes the open reflex-corner case of Corollary 16. A single mark `s_n` tends to a reflex corner `c` of `P0`, with angle `α` in `{5π/4, 3π/2, 7π/4}`. The other mark stays interior, with `t_n -> t0`. Use the moving corner `c_n` and the zoom angle `φ_s` from Lemma 15, with `0 <= φ_s <= α`.

### Lemma 22: the shadow of a reflex corner

**Statement.** Lemma 15 also holds with any local image of `s_n` in place of `s_n`. The images, on the unwrapped sheet at `c`, sit at angles `±φ_s + 2j·α`. Call a departure direction `ψ` in `(0, α)` **admissible** if some image is within `π` of it. Then the directions that aren't admissible form the **shadow**:
- `S = (0, φ_s - π]` when `φ_s > π`;
- `S = [φ_s + π, α)` when `φ_s < α - π`;
- `S` is empty when `α - π <= φ_s <= π`.

The shadow is an arc next to one wall, of length at most `α - π`. Codex corrected an earlier "less than": equality holds when the mark arrives along a wall, at `φ_s = 0` or `φ_s = α`. The length is at most `3π/4`, and at most `π/2` for a 270° corner.

**Proof.** The image `φ_s` covers `(φ_s - π, φ_s + π)`. The image `-φ_s` covers `(0, π - φ_s)`, which is nonempty only when `φ_s < π`. The image `2α - φ_s` covers `(2α - φ_s - π, α)`, which is nonempty only when `φ_s > α - π`. Every other image is at least `α + φ_s` or `2α - φ_s` away from the interval `(0, α)`, and both exceed `π`. Now combine the cases. If `φ_s > π`, the first image covers `(φ_s - π, α)`, and the third image's arc lies inside it. So `(0, φ_s - π]` stays uncovered. The case `φ_s < α - π` is the mirror image. Otherwise the first image alone covers `(0, α)`. So the local reflections never enlarge the admissible set. The shadow is the complement of the direct arc of Lemma 15.

### Corollary 23: what darkness forces

If `P_n` is dark, then every robust connection of `P0` from `c` to `t0` leaves `c` inside the shadow `S`. In particular:
1. If `α - π <= φ_s <= π`, then `c` has no robust connection to `t0` at all. That makes `(c, t0)` a dark pair of `P0` with one end at a cone point.
2. Otherwise, `c` reaches `t0` only through an arc of departure directions of length at most `α - π`, next to one wall.

For `t0`, being one of the finitely many points that `c` doesn't illuminate is a closed, exceptional condition. Lelièvre–Monteil–Weiss prove that finite exceptional set for a fixed polygon and source. Their Theorem 2 covers any point of the surface, and its proof treats a singular source separately (arXiv:1407.2975, §5, Theorem 11 (i)). So a cone-point source is allowed. Their paper has no statement about restricted sectors of directions or about density of directions.

### Theorem 24 (Codex): sector density on lattice surfaces

**Statement.** Let `X` be a finite translation cover of a torus, such as the unfolding of a grid room. Mark a finite set `Σ` of stopping points that includes every singularity. Suppose all branch values, all of `Σ`, the corner lift `c`, and the regular point `t` have rational torus coordinates. If one straight connection from `c` to `t` avoids `Σ`, then the departure directions of such connections are dense in the whole link of `c`, of length `2πk`. The proof is in `degeneration-review/reflex-review.md`.

**Proof outline.**
1. Refine the torus so that every marked point lies over the origin. The affine maps that fix every label have derivatives forming a finite-index subgroup `H` of `SL(2, Z)`. Each maps `c`-to-`t` connections to `c`-to-`t` connections.
2. Products of unipotent powers in `H` give hyperbolic elements whose attracting and repelling lines approximate any two rational directions.
3. A power of such a map fixes every eigenray on the cone link of `c`. Between adjacent repelling rays, which are `π` apart, it attracts everything to the attracting ray.
4. The closure of the orbit of one departure direction therefore contains a `π`-neighborhood of each of its points. So it's open and closed, and it fills the link.

**Claude's review.** Checked:
- the finite-index labeled stabilizer, and the existence of label-fixing affine lifts;
- `N_r² = 0`, the limit `A_n/n² -> ab·Δ·r·w_sᵀ` with trace `-ab·Δ²`, and the convergence of the eigenlines;
- the even shift of the `4k` alternating eigenrays, so a `2k`-th power fixes them all;
- the open-and-closed argument on the connected link.

Folding the link onto the physical sector `(0, α)` keeps density, so connections leave `c` in every open sub-arc. Accepted.

**Consequence.** For a limit configuration with rational corner and target coordinates, a nonempty set of connections can't fit inside the shadow, even at the endpoint length `α - π`. So a dark sequence that converges to such a configuration forces `c` to have no robust connection to `t0` at all. Lelièvre–Monteil–Weiss then put `t0` in a finite exceptional set.

### Conjecture 24: departure directions spread out, for real limits

**Conjecture.** Let `c` be a reflex corner of a rational polygon with real parameters, and let `t0` be an interior point that `c` illuminates robustly. Then the departure directions of robust connections from `c` to `t0` aren't contained in any closed arc of length at most `α - π` next to a wall.

Theorem 24 proves this when the corner, the target, and the blockers have rational coordinates on a lattice surface. A limit of rescaled lattice rooms needn't be rational, so the real case stays open.

**What it gives.** With Conjecture 24, a mark that tends to a reflex corner reduces to item 1 of Corollary 23. That's a dark pair at a cone point, which is the same kind of exceptional configuration as a mark at a convex 135° corner.

**Why it's plausible, and what's missing.**
- From any regular point, the directions of trajectories that hit some cone point are dense (Hubert–Schmidt). The standard proof sends a fan of rays and uses the first cone point the fan hits. That cone point needn't be `c`, so the proof gives no density for a prescribed corner and a prescribed arc.
- Vorobets shows that almost every regular point lies on closed geodesics in a dense set of directions. A cylinder through `t0` that has `c` on its boundary, on the correct side, would give a connection. But nothing forces `c` onto that boundary.
- So the gap is a "prescribed corner, prescribed sector" version of a known density theorem.

**Marks merging at a reflex corner.** Theorem 24 controls one end at a time, so it doesn't settle loops. The same analysis applies at both ends of a loop from `c` back to `c`. Darkness forces every robust loop to leave in the shadow of `s_n` or to arrive in the shadow of `t_n`, after allowing both orders. A two-ended form of Conjecture 24 would reduce this case to an exceptional configuration too.

## Toward isolation of every dark room

Step 2 of the plan needs every dark room of a shape to be isolated. Theorem 6 shows this one room at a time. This section records the structure that all four certified rooms share, and what it reduces the general question to. It's Claude's work from 2026-11-01. Codex reviewed it the same day in `isolation-review/findings.md`, and its corrections are in.

### Lemma 25: parity rooms are dark

Let `A = s + 2Z²`, with `t` in `A`. Assume every wall reflection preserves `Z²` and `A`. The canonical tiling does: its walls lie on lines `x = k`, `y = k`, and `x ± y = 2k`. A diagonal on `x + y = 1` would not, since it sends `(0,0)` to `(1,1)`. Call the room a *parity room* when every non-A lattice point of the closed room is a corner. A parity room is dark.

*Proof.* This is the argument of Lemma 1, written by Codex for Jain's room in `jain28/darkness.md`. Unfold a path from `s` to `t`. Reflections in tiling lines keep A points as A points, so the unfolded target is `s + 2g·w`, with `w` primitive and `g >= 1`. The point `s + w` is a non-A lattice point inside the segment. It folds back to a non-A lattice point of the closed room, which is a corner. That stops the path. ∎

Four certified rooms are parity rooms: the 24-wall room, Wolecki's pinch room, the 34-wall room, and Jain's room. A direct check of each room's lattice points confirms this. **Your 22-wall room is not one**, as Codex pointed out. Its non-A points `(-1,0)`, `(1,0)`, `(2,1)`, and `(2,3)` aren't corners. Its darkness uses reflected copies and retracing instead. Of its 147 events, 96 have the corner at the midpoint. The other 51 have it at 1/4 or 3/4 of the way, with unfolded target `s + 4w` and a transparent non-A point in between. So parity rooms are a subclass, not every known mechanism.

### Lemma 26: every event sits at a midpoint

In a parity room, each event of Lemma 5 has unfolded target `s + 2w`, with `w` primitive, and its corner is the fold of the midpoint `s + w`.

*Proof.* An event path meets exactly one corner. The lattice points of the segment from `s` to `s + 2g·w` are `s + k·w`, and they're non-A exactly when `k` is odd. So the segment holds `g` non-A points, each a corner, and `g = 1`. ∎

All 932 events in the four parity-room certificates have this form. Codex checked each half vector exactly. That covers 168, 179, 403, and 182 events respectively, with `R` = 12, 18, 18, and 18.

**Consequence: events are combinatorial.** Every primitive direction `w` from `s` reaches a corner `c_w` at unfolded distance `|w|`, because no lattice point lies before it. Past `c_w` on side `σ`, the next lattice point is `s + 2w`, an A point. An event occurs exactly when that point folds to `t`. If it folds elsewhere, the path continues to `s + 3w`, a corner, and stops. So a parity room's events come from folding, with no search for the corner. The folding still needs a labeled unfolding: which sheet of the corner the path is on, and which side continuation is admissible. Regular 45° and 90° corners reverse the path, and a contact vertex has two labels at one point.

### Lemma 27: the taut-string form

Write `γ1` for the leg from `s` to `c` and `γ2` for the leg from `c` to `t`. By Lemma 26, both legs have holonomy `w = (p, q)` and length `a = |w|`. Let `δω = δx + i·δy` be the first-order change of the periods of the unfolded surface. Then

    g_e(δ) = −σ_e · ⟨p·δy − q·δx, η_e⟩,   η_e = [γ2] − [γ1],

with `σ_e` the side exported by `rigidity45.py`. Here `η_e` is a relative homology class with zero holonomy, and `∂η_e = s + t − 2c`, for the actual lifts of `s`, `c`, `t` on the unfolded surface. Codex corrected the sign. With `w = (1,0)`, `δhol γ1 = 0`, and `δhol γ2 = (0,1)`, the exported derivative is `−1`.

*Proof.* Put `C = hol γ1` and `A = hol γ1 + hol γ2`. The exported functional is `σ_e · d(A × C)`. At the base, `C = w` and `A = 2w`, so `d(A × C) = −w × (δhol γ2 − δhol γ1)`. Then use `δhol γ = ⟨δω, γ⟩`. ∎

So `g_e / a²` is the first-order change of the bend angle at `c`. Darkness requires the angle on the incoming side to stay at least `π`. That angle is measured on the developed sheet of the corner, not in the room: events also occur at convex 135° corners. Each event is a string pulled taut around a corner, and isolation asks whether any deformation can keep every string taut.

GL(2,R) changes `δω = Aω` give `⟨Aω, η_e⟩ = A·hol(η_e) = 0`. Only some of them are room deformations. With diagonal walls, those are translations and scaling. Orthogonal rooms also allow independent axis scales. This matches the kernels in Theorem 6.

### What the data rules out

Three cheaper mechanisms fail on all four rooms:

- **No two-sided events.** No event passes straight through a corner, so no event gives a linear equality.
- **No cancelling pairs.** No two events have opposite functionals, even modulo closure. All 932 rays give distinct functionals.
- **No natural weights.** The combinations `Σ w_e·g_e` with `w_e` = 1, `1/a²`, `1/a³`, or `1/|g_e|` leave residuals of 3 to 31 percent of `Σ w_e·|g_e|`. Random positive weights leave 4 to 11 percent. So no visible integral identity forces the certificate.

The certificates are global combinations. A general proof has to show that the cone generated by the vectors `σ_e·(p_e·η_e^y − q_e·η_e^x)` is all of `(V/sym)*`, where `V` is the room's deformation space. A log-log fit of `|g_e|/a` against `a` gives exponents 0.22 to 0.64 on the four rooms, with `a` up to 32. That doesn't prove anything, as Codex showed. A fit isn't a bound, and a bound on the row isn't a bound on `η_e`. Even a true sublinear bound says nothing about signs. Rows `m·e_j` in dimension 3 grow like `sqrt(a)` and have full rank, but their positive cone is only an orthant. The Kontsevich–Zorich deviation theory (Forni, arXiv:math/0405031) concerns generic flows, not these rational saddle connections. It's a possible direction, with no bridge yet.

**The missing sign theorem.** For every nonzero room variation `δ`, modulo symmetries, some admissible event `e` has `g_e(δ) < 0`. Compactness of the unit sphere then gives a finite certificate, as in Theorem 6. For parity rooms this would be a real advance. It would still leave out mechanisms like your room's.

### What the 327 certificates show about the sign theorem

Claude analyzed the existing `R = 12` certificates on 2026-11-01, with no new search. Scripts are in `parity-enum/analysis/`. Radii are `max(|p|,|q|)` of the direction.

- **No short local certificate.** Positive spanning first holds at radius 7 to 11, and full rank at radius 5 to 9. No family of short events works for every room.
- **Wall moves survive longest.** Just below each room's certifying radius, the surviving deformation found by an LP always moves walls. In 18 rooms it fixes the marks; it never fixes the walls.
- **The mark part has a closed form.** In all 44,137 events, the gradient on `s` is perpendicular to the direction `w`. The gradient on `t` is `Rᵀ` times the gradient on `s`, where `R` is the linear part of the unfolding, an element of the 8-element symmetry group of the square lattice. So for mark moves alone, `g_e = ±σ_e·(δs + R_e·δt) × w_e`. In 300 rooms, all 8 classes of `R` have event directions that positively span the plane; in the other 27, 7 classes do. Two such classes whose quotient is a rotation force `δs = δt = 0`. This is first-order mark isolation, which Wolecki's finiteness theorem already implies, so it's not new.
- **Corners next to the marks suffice.** Take only the events whose corner is one of the 8 lattice points around `s` or around `t`. In all 327 rooms, these events alone have full rank and positively span. They make up 62% to 67% of the corners, but carry 89% of the events.

So the refined target is the sign theorem for events at the ring corners. Every parity room has the same local geometry there. Each mark sits in a full block of 8 tiles. Its four axis neighbors are forced 270° corners, and each one forces one tile in the next block. The paths themselves are long and depend on the whole room, so this isn't yet a local statement.

### What it changes

- Darkness of a parity room needs no ray tracing, and neither do its events. A parity room's certificate can be built by folding.
- Not every known dark room is a parity room: yours isn't. Real (non-lattice) dark rooms of a shape are a separate open question, and Lemma 25 says nothing about them.
