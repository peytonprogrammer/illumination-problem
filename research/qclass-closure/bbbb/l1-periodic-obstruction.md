Advances: L1 — the exceptional specified pair on an arithmetic unfolding. Every lattice mark already has finite affine orbit. The nine-reflex count for R22 uses the branching of its particular fold. The missing implication is from darkness of the specified pair to such branching or to a different obstruction with a comparable cost.

### What the classification gives

Let Q be the double of the room and X its orientation cover (the four-copy billiard unfolding). Mark in X the entire inverse image F of the physical corners, including the smooth lifts of convex corners. Fix one lift s of S. The exact criterion is that the pair is dark iff for **each** of the four lifts t of T there is no straight segment from s to t whose interior avoids F. Unfolding gives one direction of the equivalence and folding gives the other. Fixing s loses nothing by the copy symmetries. A symmetry of a single ordered lift pair alone cannot certify this intersection of four conditions. Wolecki's Theorem 1.2(i) proves finiteness of dark physical pairs for each fixed rational polygon, including this marked-corner convention. [Wolecki, §1, Theorem 1.2(i)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1905.09358)

Hubert–Schmoll–Troubetzkoy's Theorem 2 gives finiteness of dark targets for a fixed source on a Veech surface. Their Theorem 8 requires that the reduced surface square have just the diagonal and a specified involution graph as nonparallel invariant subspaces. Their proof shows this hypothesis forces non-arithmeticity. Every lattice room has an arithmetic unfolding, so Theorem 8 does not apply here. In its own domain the periodic-pair alternative leaves individual pairs to decide. It gives no general implication that the target is an affine image of the source. [HST, Theorems 2 and 8](https://arxiv.org/pdf/math/0602394)

Apisa–Wright's Theorem 1.4 concerns periodic markings **over the orientation-cover locus of a connected component of a quadratic stratum**. Outside the three exceptional strata listed there it restricts them to fixed points of the orientation involution J, with an additional fixed-point possibility in hyperelliptic components. Our Q lies in the genus-zero stratum with r simple zeros and r+4 simple poles. Its orientation involution has sphere quotient, branches at all 2r+4 corners, and gives X genus r+1 by Riemann–Hurwitz. For genus at least two J is itself the hyperelliptic involution of X. It interchanges lifts over the same regular point of Q and fixes no lift of an interior room mark. A lattice realization has a closed arithmetic orbit. A marking periodic over this orbit need not be periodic as Q varies through its stratum. Applying the full-locus fixed-point test to these lattice marks would incorrectly discard R22 itself. [Apisa–Wright, Theorem 1.4 and §5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1708.03411)

### Why periodicity costs zero reflex corners

Take the absolute period lattice Λ of X in square coordinates. It is a finite-index sublattice of the integer grid (X is a finite translation cover of the square torus). Integration from a marked corner o defines a finite map π to the torus with lattice Λ. Choose N with Nℤ² contained in Λ. Every corner and every lattice lift projects into the finite N-torsion subgroup because paths between grid vertices develop with integral displacement. An affine automorphism preserving F acts downstairs by its derivative, which preserves Λ, followed by translation by π(g o), an N-torsion point. Thus the inverse image of N-torsion is a finite invariant set containing all lattice lifts. In particular every ordered pair of lattice lifts has finite diagonal orbit, even when the physical marks see one another directly.

Even requiring a nonidentity symmetry exchanging the marks costs zero reflexes. In the rectangle [0,4] × [0,2] the half-turn exchanges (1,1) and (3,1), lifts to the unfolding, and their horizontal segment is legal. Finite orbit also supplies no group element taking its first coordinate to its second. A count must therefore constrain **what blocks all the paths**, beyond the order of an involution or the periodicity of its endpoints.

### A count that does predict the R22 obstruction

For a pillowcase factor Q → N of degree k, suppose N has z points of angle 3π. Every preimage of such a point has angle 3π times its local degree. The room double has maximum cone angle 3π, so every such fiber consists of k distinct reflex corners. Consequently r ≥ kz. This needs neither regularity of the cover nor a deck transformation exchanging endpoints. In particular k ≥ 3 and z ≥ 3 force W = 2r+4 ≥ 22. The stronger exclusion for separated R22-type parity folds with smaller degrees has additional hypotheses on the three off-classes and the marked images. [Reviewed fold count and hypotheses](fold-converse.md)

In the project's R22 the three reflected five-square staircases give k=3 and z=3. The fold sends (0,0) and (2,2) to distinct smooth sites a=(0,0) and b=(2,−2). These are the staircase's only noncorner lattice sites. A hypothetical connecting path cannot be axis-parallel because reflection preserves a zero velocity component and the marks differ on both axes. Each of its finitely many legs therefore meets each seam transversely at most once. Folding gives a finite sequence of ordinary reflections and possible velocity reversals at convex staircase vertices that are images of smooth upstairs seam ends. Reflex vertices remain forbidden.

In the staircase truncate at the first arrival at b and start at the last visit to a before it. A first reversal would retrace the ordinary prefix by reversibility. It could not encounter b on that prefix and would revisit a, contrary to truncation. The retained path is therefore ordinary and avoids every vertex. Its development ends at a nonzero even vector B. Dividing B by its largest common power of two gives a strictly intermediate lattice point with an odd coordinate. Reflection in integer walls preserves parity, so this point folds to a staircase vertex, contradicting ordinariness. This proves darkness of the physical pair. The obstructing point can occur at different dyadic depths for different developments. [Project staircase proof, parity and seam lemmas](../../illumination-publication/repository/preprints/twenty-two-wall/manuscript.tex)

### What remains on the marked cover U

For same-parity marks let U be the normalized union of the relevant off-diagonal components of the fiber product of two copies of Q over the pillowcase. A sufficient witness is a straight trajectory from compatible marked lifts to a regular diagonal coincidence over an off-class, with the two projections meeting at equal travel time and opposite incoming velocities and avoiding forbidden corners. Reversing the second projection then completes the billiard path. Periodicity on X controls neither this trajectory nor the fiber in which it meets the diagonal. In R22 the marked coincidence budget is 24, with six regular branches all in the source class and eighteen reflex branches. Even where off-class branches exist, component membership gives no straight approach. Different-parity marks require the corresponding translated fiber product. [Marked-cover count](midpoint-necessity.md)

### The pinned eight-reflex case

In a positive chart let S=(X_i,Y_j) and T=(X_k,Y_l), where all four coordinates are prescribed wall levels. For each room in this chart these equations select one ordered pair. Thus even a bound of **one** exceptional unordered pair per room would allow this particular pair to be exceptional. Lelièvre–Monteil–Weiss give per-source finiteness on a translation surface in Theorem 2 and on a rational polygon in Corollary 3. Theorem 2 also allows isolated exceptional pairs in the surface square. Wolecki's finiteness for the whole polygon still allows the pinned singleton. [LMW, Theorem 2 and Corollary 3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1407.2975) · [Wolecki, Theorem 1.2(i)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1905.09358)

Pinning, same parity and exchange by a half-turn can coexist at exactly eight reflex corners. Here are two symbolic controls. Start with the square of side L=2M for an integer M≥7. Put u=M−2. Cut a rectangular notch into the top over [u,u+1] down to height f, and take its half-turn image at the bottom. Cut a unit-deep notch into the left wall over heights [3,4] and its half-turn image at the right. The four mutually disjoint indentations each contribute two reflex corners. Both S=(u,3) and T=(L−u,L−3) are interior and pinned by notch wall levels. The half-turn exchanges them and their coordinate differences are even.

* With f=L−1 the whole chord ST stays between heights 3 and L−3 and between abscissas u and L−u. It misses all four notches. This gives a lit pinned pair with the proposed symmetry.
* With f=4 the chord has slope m=(L−6)/4>1. It meets the floor of the top notch at abscissa u+1/m, strictly inside its width. Thus even pinning, periodicity, the half-turn and failure of direct visibility can hold together in a positive eight-reflex chart. Both controls are lit for every tested M from 7 through 12. For f=4 the reviewer verified corridor paths with 4–10 reflections by two exact tracers. At M=7 the developed displacement is 4(3,2), with four reflections. This finite check leaves the general f=4 reflected-path question outside the control calculation. [d216, claim 6](../../illumination-generalization-2026-11-06/fable/directions-2026-11-09/d216-review-periodic-obstruction/review.md)

The **first open case for the compactness lift** is a potentially exceptional pinned, nondirect pair in a positive eight-reflex chart. The two controls establish why counting periodic marks or endpoint symmetries cannot decide that case. For same-parity marks satisfying the candidate-count hypotheses, it suffices to exhibit a compatible straight approach on U to one regular off-class coincidence. The precise gap in a proof by contradiction is that failure of all such approaches has not been shown to force a branched quotient of sufficient cost. Theorem S's separate wall-count threshold r≤2 is unchanged. [d215 review](../../illumination-generalization-2026-11-06/fable/directions-2026-11-09/d215-review-ratio-compactness/review.md)
