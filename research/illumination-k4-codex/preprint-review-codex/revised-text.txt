Abstract
We give exact coordinates for a strictly simple orthogonal polygon with 22 maximal sides and
two distinct interior points that cannot be joined by any ﬁnite billiard trajectory avoiding polygon
vertices. The room is assembled from three reﬂected copies of a ﬁve-square staircase. Folding
a hypothetical trajectory onto the staircase reduces the problem to an elementary lattice-parity
obstruction. Four smooth boundary points of the assembled room project to staircase corners;
we show explicitly that reﬂection at these exceptional points produces only trajectory reversal and
cannot evade the obstruction. The construction uses neither interior mirror partitions nor boundary
self-contacts. We make no claim that 22 sides is minimal.

Introduction

In the polygonal illumination problem, light rays travel in straight lines and reﬂect specularly from
the sides of a polygonal room. A ray that reaches a vertex is terminated. Two interior points are said
to illuminate each other if some ﬁnite trajectory with these reﬂection rules connects them.
Tokarsky gave a polygonal example with a non-illuminating pair of points using 26 sides [1]. Castro
exhibited a 24-sided example, as noted in [2]. Wolecki presented a 22-sided example and established
the upper bound of 22 sides for the unrestricted polygonal setting [2, Theorem 1.3]. Wolecki also
mentions an independent, unpublished 22-sided example due to Thierry Monteil. Thus the present
note does not improve the known numerical upper bound.
Our purpose is to describe an explicit strictly simple, orthogonal 22-gon with a short elementary proof.
“Strictly simple” means that the boundary is a Jordan polygonal curve: it has no repeated vertices,
crossed edges, or self-contacts. Only the exterior boundary reﬂects light; the dashed segments in the
construction are open passages, not mirrors. The argument is self-contained and requires no general
theory of translation surfaces.
Theorem 1 (Main result). There exists a strictly simple orthogonal polygon Q with exactly 22 maximal
sides, without holes or interior mirror partitions, containing two distinct points S, T ∈ int(Q) such
that no ﬁnite billiard trajectory avoiding the vertices of Q connects S to T .

The lattice obstruction on the staircase

The next lemma concerns ordinary billiard motion in P , reﬂecting only at smooth boundary points
and never touching a vertex. Paths have ﬁnitely many nonzero straight legs. At each intermediate
smooth-wall contact the incoming and outgoing legs satisfy the specular reﬂection rule.
2

Lemma 1 (Parity obstruction). There is no ﬁnite corner-avoiding billiard path in P from a = (0, 0)
to b = (2, −2).
Proof. First inspect the integer points in P . There are no interior integer points. Its only integer
boundary points that are not vertices are a and b. More explicitly, its seven convex corners are
(−1, −1), (−1, 0), (0, −2), (1, −3), (1, 0), (2, −3), (2, −1),
and its three reﬂex corners are
(0, −1), (1, −2), (1, −1).
Suppose an ordinary billiard path from a to b exists. Unfold its ﬁnitely many reﬂections, reﬂecting the
staircase instead of bending the path. The result is a single straight segment starting at a = (0, 0) and
ending at an image B of b. Reﬂection across any wall x = c or y = d, where c, d ∈ Z, preserves both
coordinate parities. Since b has even coordinates, B ∈ (2Z)2 . Further, B 6= (0, 0) because unfolding
preserves the positive length of the path.
Write B = 2r z, where r ≥ 1 and z ∈ Z2 has at least one odd coordinate; choose r maximal. Then
z is a lattice point strictly between 0 and B on the unfolded segment. Fold z back to P along the
proposed itinerary. All inverse unfolding maps are compositions of reﬂections in integer-coordinate
lines and therefore preserve integrality and coordinate parities. The image of z is an integer point of
P with an odd coordinate. It is neither a nor b, hence must be a vertex of P . This would make the
original path hit a vertex, a contradiction.
Remark 1. The lemma is valid for arbitrary ﬁnite path length, direction, and number of reﬂections. The
obstruction arises from the parity of an unfolded endpoint, not from a bounded search over directions.

What happens at the seams?

A billiard trajectory in Q need not fold into an ordinary billiard trajectory in P at every instant.
The exceptions are precisely the smooth boundary points of Q lying at the ends of open seams. It is
important to account for them rather than treating them as forbidden corners of Q.
Away from the seams, each branch of f is an isometry. A reﬂection at a smooth exterior wall of
Q thus becomes an ordinary reﬂection at a smooth wall of P , unless that point is a seam endpoint.
A straight passage across the interior of either seam also becomes an ordinary reﬂection in P : the
adjacent folding maps agree at the seam and reverse the normal component of velocity.

the corner reversals of Lemma 2.

Why reversals do not create a connecting path

Lemma 3 (Reversals do not help). There is no ﬁnite path from a to b in P obtained by ordinary
billiard reﬂections at smooth walls together with the additional rule that a path may reverse its velocity
and retrace itself at a convex corner. Reﬂex corners remain forbidden.
Proof. Suppose such a path exists. Stop it at its ﬁrst arrival at b, and retain only the subpath beginning
at its last visit to a before that arrival. The resulting path γ starts at a, ends at b, and contains no
interior occurrence of either marked point. There are ﬁnitely many such visits because the path has
ﬁnitely many nonzero straight legs. This truncation takes place in P : a visit to a counts regardless of
which preimage of a the original path visits in Q.
Assume γ has a corner reversal, and choose the ﬁrst one. Up to that event, motion consists entirely
of ordinary billiard reﬂections. Ordinary billiard motion is deterministic and reversible. Once the
ﬁrst reversal takes place, the path must retrace the entire preceding route in reverse order, ultimately
reaching a. It cannot reach b during this retracing, since the corresponding point would already have
occurred before the reversal, contradicting the ﬁrst-arrival choice of b. Thus the path revisits a before
reaching b, contradicting the last-visit choice of a.
Hence γ has no reversals and is an ordinary corner-avoiding billiard path from a to b, contrary to
Lemma 1.

Proof of Theorem 1. The explicit construction and boundary cycle establish that Q is a strictly simple
orthogonal polygon with 22 maximal sides, no holes, and S, T ∈ int(Q).
Suppose a ﬁnite billiard trajectory joins S to T in Q. It cannot be axis-parallel: reﬂecting in a
horizontal or vertical wall preserves the property that one velocity component vanishes, whereas S
and T diﬀer in both coordinates. Consequently every straight leg meets either seam transversely, if at
all. Since the trajectory has ﬁnitely many legs and each leg crosses each seam at most once, its folding
by f splits into ﬁnitely many straight segments and events.
By the seam analysis, the image is a ﬁnite path from a = f (S) to b = f (T ) following the rules of
Lemma 3. That lemma forbids such a path. Therefore no ﬁnite corner-avoiding billiard trajectory
joins S and T .

Discussion and scope

This example uses a connected, simply connected, axis-aligned room whose 22 boundary edges are
all genuine exterior mirrors. The two marked points are interior points, rather than points of the
reﬂective boundary. The proof’s key components are a mod-2 obstruction after unfolding and an
explicit treatment of the four points where a smooth wall of the assembled room maps to a corner of
the base staircase.
The theorem provides an explicit construction, not a new upper bound on the unrestricted illumination
problem: a 22-side upper bound was already given in [2]. A complete historical claim about priority among strictly simple orthogonal examples would require checking other constructions, including
unpublished work. The proof is independent of the computational searches that helped identify the
example. The reversal principle is consistent with the usual continuation through removable corners;
see [2, Remark 2.2]. The claimed contribution here is the explicit orthogonal construction and its
self-contained proof, rather than a new general reversal principle.
