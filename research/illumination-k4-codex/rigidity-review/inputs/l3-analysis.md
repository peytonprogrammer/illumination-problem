# L3 analysis: the case with many transparent points

Status: working memo, 2026-10-31. Owner of the investigation: Peyton Wang. Claude drafts; Codex reviews.

## Summary

The bounded theorem caps the number of transparent points `k`, and `k` measures the room's size, not its complexity. Integer scaling leaves a room dark and keeps `W` fixed, while it multiplies `k`. So an unbounded proof has to work with a quantity that scaling leaves unchanged.

This memo proposes that quantity: the unfolded translation surface, with its corners marked. For a room with `W` walls, the surface's genus and its number of marked points are bounded by functions of `W`. That makes the problem finite-dimensional for each `W`.

Lemmas 1 to 3 are proved here. Conjecture 4 would reduce the open case to the bounded theorem, plus a finite check. It is not proved.

## Lemma 1: the unfolded surface of an orthogonal room

**Statement.** Let `P` be a strictly simple orthogonal room with `W` walls, `c` convex corners, and `r` reflex corners. Glue 4 reflected copies of `P` along matching walls, using the group generated by the reflections in `x` and in `y`. The result is a closed translation surface `X` with these properties:

1. `X` is tiled by unit squares, so it is a square-tiled surface, with area `4 * area(P)`.
2. Each corner of `P` lifts to exactly one point of `X`. A convex corner lifts to a regular point. A reflex corner lifts to a cone point of angle `6π`.
3. `X` has genus `g = r + 1 = (W - 2) / 2`.
4. An interior point of `P` has 4 lifts, and a point inside a wall has 2.
5. Interior points `s` and `t` form a dark pair exactly when the following holds. For each lift `t'` of `t`, every straight segment in `X` from `s` to `t'` meets a lift of a corner before it reaches `t'`.

**Proof.**
- Around a corner of angle `π/2`, the 4 copies supply a total angle of `2π`, so they close up to one regular point.
- Around a corner of angle `3π/2`, the copies supply `6π`, which forms one cone point.
- Gauss–Bonnet gives `2g - 2 = Σ(cone angle / 2π - 1) = 2r`, so `g = r + 1`.
- An orthogonal polygon has `c - r = 4`, so `W = 2r + 4`.
- Item 5 is the standard unfolding. A billiard path becomes a straight segment in `X`, and the stopping rule at corners becomes the condition on corner lifts.

**Check on your room.** It has 13 convex and 9 reflex corners, area 15, and genus 10. Its surface `X` has area 60 and 22 marked points.

## Lemma 2: the 45° case

For a room with diagonal walls, use all 16 elements of the symmetry group of the square. A corner of angle `aπ/4` lifts as follows:

| `a` | Corner angle | Points over the corner | Cone angle of each |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 45° | 2 | `2π` |
| 2 | 90° | 4 | `2π` |
| 3 | 135° | 2 | `6π` |
| 5 | 225° | 2 | `10π` |
| 6 | 270° | 4 | `6π` |
| 7 | 315° | 2 | `14π` |

**Proof.** The 16 copies supply a total angle of `16 · aπ/4 = 4aπ`. A corner angle of `π · m/n` in lowest terms has cone angle `2πm`. Dividing the total by the cone angle gives the count.

So `X` has at most `4W` marked points, and its genus is bounded by a linear function of `W`. Darkness has the same form as Lemma 1, item 5.

## Lemma 3: scaling doesn't change the surface

**Statement.** Scale `P` by a positive integer `M`. The new surface is `X` itself, tiled by squares of side `1/M`. The marked points, `s`, and the lifts of `t` all keep their places.

**Proof.** Scaling commutes with reflection in lines through the origin. It maps the gluing of `P` onto the gluing of `MP`.

**Consequence.** Darkness is a property of the marked surface `(X, corners, s, lifts of t)` up to scaling. The tiling only says which surfaces arise. That's why bounds on `k` can't settle the general case, and why bounds on genus and marked points can.

Lemma 3 also explains Codex's finding about orthogonal rooms. The map `(x, y) -> (Mx, y)` commutes with both axis reflections, so it maps `X` to an affinely equivalent surface. It preserves straight lines and lattice points, and therefore darkness.

## A known constraint: marks are isolated

Lelièvre, Monteil, and Weiss proved that every point of a translation surface illuminates all but finitely many points. The paper is "Everything is illuminated," *Geometry & Topology* 20 (2016). Check the exact statement before you cite it.

For a fixed room and a fixed `s`, only finitely many `t` are dark. So you can't slide `t` continuously and keep it dark. Darkness is a rigid, lattice-type coincidence. That matches every dark room in the literature.

## Parameter space for a fixed shape

Fix a **shape** `τ`: the cyclic sequence of turn angles at the corners. With `W <= 21`, there are finitely many shapes.

- Rooms of shape `τ` are given by their edge lengths `ℓ in R^W`. These satisfy two linear closing equations, plus open inequalities that keep the room simple. Call this region `K_τ`. It is a polyhedral cone.
- Add the 4 coordinates of `s` and `t`. The full parameter space is a cone of dimension `W + 2`.
- Fix a reflection word `w`, the sequence of walls a ray hits. The copy of `t` that `w` gives depends linearly on the parameters. So does the condition that the segment from `s` meets each wall in its interior. The set lit through `w` is therefore an open polyhedral cone.
- The dark set `D_τ` is what remains after removing the countably many lit cones. It is closed and invariant under scaling. By the result of Lelièvre, Monteil, and Weiss, it has empty interior.

## Conjecture 4: rigidity

**Conjecture.** For each shape `τ` with `W <= 21`, `D_τ` is contained in a finite union of rational linear subspaces of positive codimension. On each one, darkness is a property of the subspace, not of the particular point.

**Why it would close L3.**
- Each subspace has a smallest integral point, up to scaling.
- If those smallest points all have `k <= 4`, the bounded theorem rules them out.
- Otherwise, the leftover finite list of subspaces could be checked one at a time with exact certificates.

**Evidence.**
- Every known dark room comes from an exact lattice coincidence. Tokarsky's rooms, Wolecki's rooms, and your room all fit this pattern.
- Codex's strip test agrees. Inserting a strip into a room moves oblique crossings, and the room becomes lit. So darkness doesn't survive a one-parameter stretch that isn't a symmetry.

**Main risk.** The lit set is a union of infinitely many cones. Their boundaries could pile up and leave a dark set that isn't a finite union of subspaces. To rule that out, you'd need a uniform bound on the reflection words that matter for each `τ`.


## Lemma 5: blocking at a corner breaks one-sidedly

**Setting.** Fix a shape `τ`. Deform a dark configuration along a line `θ0 + λθ` in parameter space, where `θ` keeps the polygon closed. Take a billiard path from `s` to `t`, at `λ = 0`, that touches exactly one corner `c` and otherwise reflects at wall interiors. Call it an **event**. Unfold the path. On the side `σ` of `c` that the path passes, `s`, the image `c'`, and the image `t'_σ` are collinear. All three move linearly with the parameters.

**Statement.** Let `f = ∇ det(t'_σ - s, c' - s)`, signed so that a valid path on side `σ` needs `det < 0` for left and `det > 0` for right. Write the result as a condition `f·θ >= 0` for darkness to survive. If `f·θ < 0`, the room is lit for all small `λ > 0`.

**Proof.** Every other contact of the path is a transversal hit inside a wall, or the arrival at `t`. Both are open conditions. Near an orthogonal corner, the local reflection pattern of a ray passing at distance `η` doesn't depend on `η`, so the side-`σ` continuation stays valid. `det(λ) = λ(f·θ) + O(λ²)`, so the segment passes `c` on side `σ` for small `λ > 0`.

At a convex 90° corner the unfolded surface is regular, so both sides give the same continuation. The condition becomes `f·θ = 0`.

## Theorem 6: the 22-wall room is locally rigid

**Statement.** There is a neighborhood of your room, with its marks, in the parameter space of its shape. Every configuration in that neighborhood is lit, except the images of your room under maps `(x, y) -> (ax + b, cy + d)` with `a, c > 0`.

**Proof.**
1. `rigidity/rigidity_check.py` traces rays from `s` in every primitive direction `(p, q)` with `|p|, |q| <= 12`. The tracing uses exact rationals and branches on both sides of each reflex corner. It finds 147 events and no lit path. All 147 events are at reflex corners.
2. On the 26-dimensional closure subspace, the 147 functionals have rank 22. That equals 26 minus the 4 symmetry directions, and every functional vanishes on those directions.
3. `rigidity/posspan.py` finds a strictly positive rational combination of the functionals that equals zero, and checks it exactly. File: `rigidity/posspan-R12.json`. A set of vectors with this property positively spans its linear span. So for every `θ` outside the symmetry directions, some event has `f·θ < 0`, and Lemma 5 lights the room.
4. The set of unit directions modulo symmetries is compact, so `max_e(-f_e·θ)` has a positive lower bound on it. The second-order terms are uniformly bounded over finitely many events. Together these give one neighborhood for all directions.

**Check.** `rigidity/validate.py` perturbs the room along 6 random closure-preserving directions with `λ = 10^-5`. It traces each event's predicted path in the perturbed room with exact rationals. All 881 predictions agree, both lit and blocked.

With directions up to 6 only, the rank is 22, but no positive dependency exists. Small directions alone don't certify rigidity.

## What Theorem 6 means for L3

- Your room is an isolated point of its shape's dark set, up to axis-affine maps. This proves Conjecture 4 locally at your room. In particular, the strip insertions Codex tried can't preserve darkness, at any small size.
- The same certificate works for any dark room. It is a fast, exact, local test: about 1 second per room.
- The global version is still open. It would need every dark point of `D_τ` to be rigid, plus control of `D_τ` near degenerate shapes. Near a degenerate shape, some edge becomes short compared to the others, which is where rooms with many transparent points live.
- Paper route for the degenerate end: when an edge shrinks, the room converges to a room of a simpler shape. A robust lit path of the limit room lights every nearby room. So dark rooms can pile up only at limits that are dark in a weak sense, with fewer walls or with contacts. That suggests an induction on `W`.
