# An isolated zero–pole boundary cannot be cylinder rigid

**Verdict for review.** In the three-zero problem the periodic-direction requirement in the [free collision mark lemma](three-zero-boundary.md) is automatic. Every arithmetic quadratic sphere in Q(1²,−1⁶) has a periodic direction with at least two intrinsic cylinders. Consequently a hypothetical rational rank-two locus in Q(1³,−1⁷) cannot have a cylinder-rigid boundary divisor given by just one zero–pole collision, with connected normalization and only the collision position retained. The existence of such a divisor for a hypothetical **minimal** locus is the remaining issue. No three-zero classification follows yet.

## The two cylinder directions

Let Y be an arithmetic quadratic sphere in Q(1^s,−1^(s+4)), s≥1. Use intrinsic maximal cylinders downstairs. In a periodic direction with more than one cylinder we are done. Suppose instead that the horizontal direction is periodic with a single cylinder, of circumference C and height H.

Cut the sphere along the core circle. Each side is a disk. In each disk the horizontal critical graph is connected and is a tree: the half-cylinder is a collar of the cut circle, with its other rim identified along the critical graph. The disk retracts onto this graph. Each edge is traversed twice by the rim, whose length is C, so the sum of its metric edge lengths is C/2 on either side. The trivalent vertices are the simple zeros; the leaves are the simple poles. A tree with k trivalent vertices has k+2 leaves. If k≥1, the pendant edges at those leaves are all distinct.

Choose the tree with more zeros (either in a tie), so k≥⌈s/2⌉. Pick its shortest pendant edge and call its leaf P. Its length e is at most C/[2(k+2)]. Choose any leaf P′ on the other tree. A horizontal shear can align their two rim positions vertically. Equivalently, in the original cylinder use the transverse slope obtained by joining those positions across its height. The alignment changes neither C nor H. With the aligned leaves at coordinate zero on the two rims, the two sides of each pendant edge have parameters +u and −u on the rim. They are glued by a half-turn. Let e′ be the length of the pendant edge at P′ and m=min(e,e′). If the second tree is a single segment, take e′=C/2.

For 0<u<m, the vertical leaf at +u crosses the cylinder, returns along −u after the rim identification, and closes at the other rim. These leaves sweep a cylinder of circumference 2H and area 2Hm. At u=0 the limiting leaf meets both poles. At u=m it meets a zero: on a nontrivial tree the first vertex from a leaf is a zero, and a single-segment tree has length C/2>m. Thus this is a maximal intrinsic cylinder. Its area fraction satisfies

    2Hm/(CH) ≤ 1/(k+2) ≤ 1/(⌈s/2⌉+2).

In particular at s=2 it occupies at most one third of Y. The aligned direction is periodic on the whole arithmetic surface: after putting the pillowcase lattice in standard coordinates, take the initial direction horizontal; rim vertex offsets and H are rational in these coordinates, so the alignment is a rational shear. Equivalently the pole-to-pole segment develops to a rational lattice direction on the base. The positive-area complement contains another cylinder. Affine pullback gives the asserted direction on the original Y. On the orientation double, the lifts of distinct open quadratic cylinders give distinct J-orbits of maximal cylinders: the sole extra continuation across a pole-to-pole folded rim joins a lift to its J-image and projects into the same quadratic cylinder. In particular the pocket just constructed has a zero on its other boundary and its lifted orbit cannot cover the complement.

The construction adapts Gekhtman–Marković's pole alignment in the one-zero sphere (their Proposition 6.1). Their figure suggests the shear; the two rim trees and the area estimate give the extension here. This is a genus-zero argument. One-cylinder pillowcase covers of other genera exist. [Gekhtman–Marković, §6.1](https://people.maths.ox.ac.uk/~markovic/GM-1.pdf) · [One-cylinder pillowcase covers, Theorem 1](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10711-026-01091-0)

## Consequence for the proposed induction

An isolated zero–pole collision under the divisor hypotheses above leaves an arithmetic unmarked normalization with two simple zeros. The collision supplies a free conjugate pair of regular marks on its double. Cylinder rigidity would force one J-orbit in every periodic direction by the free-mark lemma; the preceding argument supplies a direction with two. This eliminates the entire proposed boundary type. The same proof applies at s=z−1≥1 whenever the rank-two, rational, connected-divisor and marking hypotheses hold. For s=0 both rim trees are single segments, the transverse cylinder can occupy the whole pillowcase, and the argument stops exactly there.

**Remaining lemma (open).** Every minimal rational rank-two locus in Q(1³,−1⁷) has a cylinder-rigid boundary divisor with connected normalization obtained by a single zero–pole collision and no other degeneration.

Choosing an arc from a zero to a pole on the sphere does not prove this. In local periods, a single cylinder-class collapse can kill several geometrically distinct arcs when their period functionals on the locus are proportional. Coincidence of the vanishing equations still gives codimension one. Likewise a boundary divisor may be disconnected. Apisa's tree bounds and boundary codimension estimate allow both outcomes. The next structural question is whether minimality prevents the necessary proportionalities or forces a cover if all available collapses are coupled. This is stronger than having two cylinder directions. The four earlier named-room controls are already accounted for by the general lemma; no discovery computation was used.

## What coupled collisions a covering explanation predicts at z=3

There is a specific converse worth trying. Suppose the smooth three-zero quadratic sphere already covers a lower quadratic surface of degree k>1 and the locus has rank two. The target is a sphere. Its zeros are simple and unramified, and its regular points cannot be branch values, by the order formula e(a+2)−2. A four-pole target would give a torus-cover locus of rank one since all branch values are poles. Thus the target has at least one zero. If z₀ is its zero count, 3=kz₀+c₃, where c₃ counts index-three points above poles. Degree two gives c₃=0, impossible. Hence k=3, z₀=1 and c₃=0. Riemann–Hurwitz requires four simple ramification points. They lie over four *distinct* poles because the degree is three; the fifth pole is unbranched. The target stratum has dimension four, so a rank-two covering locus has full local dimension over it. [Local-degree dictionary](minimal-quotient-rigidity.md)

Follow its base zero towards a single pole, keeping all other base singularities apart. At the unbranched pole there are three simultaneous zero–pole cancellations. At any of the four other poles there is one such cancellation on the unramified sheet, accompanied by a zero–zero merger at the ramified point. Indeed with base coordinate v, varying zero a, and pole 0, the local differential is a holomorphic unit times (v−a)dv²/v. On the double local sheet v=u² the pullback is a unit times 4(u²−a)du². Its two simple zeros give a double zero at the limit. This accounts for all three moving zeros in either case. It explains geometrically why demanding an isolated cancellation of every rank-two locus would discard the very covers the classification must retain. What remains to prove is the converse for a *minimal* locus. Coupled vanishing equations alone do not produce the three sheets or their consistent permutation around the five poles.

## Effectivity if the structural exclusion succeeds

There is a quantifier gap before any corridor inequality enters. D89 starts from primitive counterexamples of **unbounded** area and selects one invariant locus supporting an unbounded subsequence. It gives no deformation line through a prescribed large finite example. The orbit of a single marked square-tiled room with lattice marks is closed and has rank one. On it the primitive torus degree is exactly n: the unfolding area is 4n and the absolute period lattice has covolume 4 after physical normalization. A lit parameter on a hypothetical higher-rank dark line eliminates that line; it places no bound on the degrees of isolated dark arithmetic orbits. This persists even if the parameter and its legal path have small rational coordinates. The L>4d and strict wall-width tests can eliminate the particular rooms or line members satisfying their geometric hypotheses. Neither test supplies a line through any specified exceptional arithmetic seed. Moreover z=3 would cover only the smallest residual cases. [Sequence-to-locus step and degree identity](dark-locus.md) · [Fixed-height reduction](accumulation.md)

One sufficient effective orbit statement, after eliminating all relevant higher-rank physical cases, would be an explicit upper bound E(r) for the primitive torus degree of the residual dark arithmetic orbits meeting normalized physical seeds. This would give an area bound E(r) directly for those seeds; no such E(r) is obtained here. Equivalently a quantitative promotion theorem could force *each* dark seed above an explicit threshold onto a positive-dimensional nonradial dark deformation. Qualitative finiteness of orbit closures supplies neither estimate.

For the direct route, an explicit bound H_sync≤(3/2−ε)n+K on unreached synchronized coincidences would give n≤(K+32)/ε at n≥120, by the reviewed lower bound. This is still the shorter implication to a finite census: a single estimate with constants, compared with a classification and a bound on the residual orbit degrees.
