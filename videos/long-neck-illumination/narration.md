# Why a longer corridor guarantees a route

## Why can a longer corridor help?

Imagine a light source in one mirrored chamber and a target in another. Connect the chambers by a straight rectangular neck, then make that neck longer. Surprisingly, after some threshold, every further real length permits a legal connecting ray. The theorem concerns each fixed opposing pair, not a universal threshold for all rooms.

## Keep the hypotheses visible

The chambers are finite orthogonal polygonal disks. Their mouths have the same positive width, and every interior mouth point is smooth before joining. The gluing makes one strictly simple room without extra contacts. Only the exterior boundary reflects. The former mouth walls become open passages, and the source and target are genuine interior points.

## The exact theorem

The chamber geometry, mouth width, and local coordinates of the two marks remain fixed. The target moves horizontally with its chamber. A finite threshold depends on those fixed data. The dimensions and marks may be arbitrary real numbers. The result does not require a rational grid, a wall-count cap, or a uniform bound on reflections.

## Density is not point attainment

The proof has two logically different stages. First it produces two finite entrance paths with compatible directions, one from each actual mark. Then an arithmetic matching argument connects them exactly. A dense orbit alone does not imply that it hits a specified point. Keeping that distinction is essential to this proof.

## The classical input

A classical theorem for rational-angle billiards says that outside countably many directions, nonsingular directional trajectories are dense in the relevant unfolding surface. Orthogonal rooms have rational angles even when their side lengths are arbitrary real numbers. We use this theorem as an input; this video does not prove the classical minimality result.

## Choose one common slope magnitude

From each fixed mark, directions that hit a corner form a countable set: each finite reflection word and corner gives at most one developed joining direction. Exclude those directions, the exceptional minimality directions, and the coordinate axes for both chambers. There is still a common admissible nonzero slope magnitude.

## Stop at the first strict mouth hit

Temporarily close each mouth and follow the chosen chamber trajectory. Density enters a small open flow box aimed through an interior mouth interval. That guarantees a strict mouth hit in finite time. Stop at the first hit, before any reflection in the temporary cap. The two finite paths begin at the actual source and target.

## A finite itinerary has an open beam

All contacts along those finite paths are smooth and transverse, away from corners. Small changes of slope preserve the same finite wall words and first-mouth conditions. Intersect the two slope neighborhoods and shrink them to a bounded open interval on one side of zero. We now have compatible finite beams, not merely infinite dense orbits.

## Replace each chamber word by a virtual point

Unfold each fixed chamber word. In its mouth chart, the source becomes a constant virtual point at minus a, alpha, and the target becomes b, beta. Both horizontal distances are positive. A sign sigma records how the reversed target beam compares with the source beam. These virtual points still represent the actual original marks.

## The mouth coordinates stay strict

For every slope tau in the common interval, the two displayed affine expressions give the physical mouth heights. Both remain strictly between zero and w. That is what excludes the neck-joint endpoints. It also preserves the positions and reflection signs needed when we reverse the target-side chamber word.

## Choose a genuine target image

Unfold the rectangular neck vertically. Its true reflection period is twice its width. Therefore the terminal target images have vertical coordinates two n w plus sigma beta. Connecting the virtual source to one of those actual target images forces a specific slope. We are choosing an image of the target, not replacing the target by a nearby point.

## An arithmetic progression of slopes

The candidate slopes form an arithmetic progression with spacing two w divided by L plus C. The admissible chamber interval has fixed positive width delta. As the neck length increases, the candidate slopes become more closely spaced. Once their spacing is smaller than delta, at least one candidate lies strictly inside the admissible interval.

## A finite threshold works for every larger length

Choose the displayed threshold. For every real L above it, the interval from L plus C times u minus c to L plus C times v minus c has length greater than two w. It contains an integer multiple of two w strictly inside. The corresponding candidate slope belongs to the common beam interval. This works for every larger length, not just a subsequence.

## The neck becomes a triangular wave

Fold the developed straight line into the physical neck using the triangular wave of period two w. Each crossing of a horizontal grid line becomes an ordinary reflection at the neck floor or ceiling. The line has finite length and nonzero finite slope, so it makes only finitely many such reflections.

## The two splices match exactly

At the left mouth, the fold begins at the source beam’s strict height and direction. At the right mouth, the phase equation gives exactly the target beam’s mouth height. Its local fold derivative is sigma, so the outgoing slope matches the reversed target word. The ray never changes direction at an interior splice.

## Every reflection is physical and smooth

The chamber pieces retain all their finite strict guards. Every neck bounce occurs strictly between the mouths, on a smooth horizontal exterior wall. The mouths themselves are crossed strictly inside their apertures. Joining the three pieces gives one finite legal path from S to the translated actual target. That completes the two-chamber argument.

## A threshold of five in one example

Here is a prescribed exact control from the proof record, not the source of the general theorem. The width is two, the common slope interval runs from minus three fifths to minus one third, and the virtual horizontal distances are both five. Delta is four fifteenths, so the displayed threshold is five.

## At L = 6, the match is exact

For length six, choose n equal to zero. The slope is minus three eighths, strictly inside the common interval. Both physical mouth heights are nine eighths, strictly between zero and two. The illustrated path has a left-chamber reflection, a neck-floor reflection, and a right-chamber reflection, and it ends at the actual target.

## Longer necks give closer candidate slopes

For length one hundred, the candidate spacing shrinks from one quarter to two fifty-fifths. More admissible target images fit inside the same fixed slope window. A longer path may have more neck bounces; the theorem guarantees existence, not a fixed reflection count. The general interval argument covers every real length above the threshold.

## The threshold is locally uniform

Shrink the slope interval so its closure stays strictly inside all the finite guards. Under small changes of chamber and mark data, the same finite words and mouth clearances then persist. That gives a locally uniform length threshold. It does not cover shrinking mouths, marks approaching corners, or arbitrary changes in chamber topology.

## A limit on long-neck dark candidates

Consequently, a sequence of dark opposing pairs cannot have neck lengths tending to infinity while the chamber, mouth, and mark data converge to nondegenerate fixed data of this type. This excludes one particular degeneration. It does not say that every large polygon decomposes into two suitable chambers and a straight neck.

## Networks require an initial witness

The same manuscript also gives a separate network theorem. Start with a fixed tree of orthogonal chambers and parallel rectangular necks. Assume their widths are commensurate, and assume one legal path already connects the actual marks at one valid parameter value. That initial witness is a hypothesis; the theorem does not manufacture it.

## Preserve the witnessed itinerary

Let the neck lengths grow along a nonnegative rational-affine rule, rescaled to integer growth coefficients. A common multiple of all neck widths supplies compatible unfolding periods. The proof shifts the witnessed chamber charts by whole periods and chooses a nearby slope. Finite strict guards persist, while the developed endpoint remains an exact image of the actual target.

## Two theorems, different logical inputs

The two-chamber theorem obtains its two entrance beams from classical minimality and then matches them arithmetically. The network theorem instead propagates a supplied compatible witness to every sufficiently large valid parameter. Branches and repeated passages are permitted, but incommensurate widths and perpendicular-neck growth are outside its statement.

## An interval turns into an exact hit

The main mechanism is now visible. Density supplies finite stable entrance beams. A long neck makes true target-image slopes close enough to enter their common interval. Folding preserves the strict mouth guards and reaches the actual target.
